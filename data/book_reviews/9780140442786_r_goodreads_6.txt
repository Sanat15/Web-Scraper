Rating: 3/5

Look, someone has to offset the parade of self-indulgent Christian praise for this relatively mundane collection of prayers and meditations. The greatest shame about this book is that if I hadn’t taken a risk and given it a moment of my curiosity, I never would have realized that most of the noise about Anselm from over-eager apologists was just flat-out bad reading and misuse. Thankfully, some scholarly exploration of my own encouraged me to check it out, and I’m glad I did.

Anselm is a much more humble and intellectually honest person than apologists make him out to be. He makes himself very clear: his intention is not to “prove” god’s existence, or even necessarily to capture all of his potential readers into his worldview or spiritual practice, but rather to offer brief segments of thought for the reader to use when and as much as they see fit. The Proslogion in particular is Anselm’s personal thought process of figuring out what “God” means to him, and his so-called “Ontological Argument” isn’t an argument, nor does Anselm regard it as such. Anselm is a fairly adept writer, purposeful and attentive in his messages. He’s an example of an often overlooked or taken for granted primary source, and while I disagree with most of his thought (unsurprisingly), the value of this work is in its historical context within the development of Christian theology.
