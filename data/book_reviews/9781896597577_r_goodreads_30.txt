Rating: 5/5

Compelling. That's the only way to describe this book. I challenge you not to see yourself and people you encounter throughout your life in this work. 

All of the negative reviews are true. Each short story is unrelated to the others, they are realistic, sweet (sometimes) but mostly sour, and even depressing. They lack beginning and certainly ending. They are only brief snapshots of normal human beings floundering in life. 

Snapshots fantastically captured in mind-reading-genius detail. 

John Donne is right, no man is an island. However, the opposite may be even more true. Everyone is an island adrift within the world and it is only when we connect that true joy is discovered and life feels worth living. 

Readers who only see the pain and disconnected awkwardness of all of Tomine's characters may fail to see how incredibly life affirming all the stories really are. They shout at the need to reach out to someone else. To find recognition, validation, intimacy in another human being. Loneliness is more than just being alone it's about knowing how similar and dependent one is within humanity and not feeling that connection. 

However, although I think that this volume is better, I recommend you read the first vol first. Not that any of these short stories are related to each other, that's not how Tomine rolls, it's just so that you can get the favor of the jicama, that you may recognize it when you taste it in these longer stories.

'No man is an iland, intire of it selfe; every man is a peece of the Continent, a part of the maine; if a clod bee washed away by the Sea, Europe is the lesse, as well as if a Promontorie were, as well as if a Mannor of thy friends or of thine owne were; any mans death diminishes me, because I am involved in Mankinde; And therefore never send to know for whom the bell tolls; It tolls for thee....' - Meditation 17, Devotions upon Emergent Occasions, John Donne, 1624.
