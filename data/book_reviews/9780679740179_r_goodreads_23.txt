Rating: 5/5

Digging pipelines is dangerous work, but it’s even more dangerous when you’ve seen a man get murdered.

I should really write taglines for a living.

ANYways. This book’s largely a portrait of what it was like digging pipelines in 1920s Texas. Men died easily, they worked hard, and nobody knew them. They were largely drifters and hoboes and the like, trying to make a few bucks to live off of and have a place to stay for a few months. They worked in horrific heat, doing jobs like dynamiting and dragging away hard-packed earth, using pneumatic jackhammers that could easily jump out of control, coating the pipes in a poisonous substance, and all sorts of other, potentially dangerous work.

Enter our narrator, Tommy, a 21-year-old orphan hobo. He’s signed onto the pipeline, what’s probably the last pipeline job of that generation, for a second round of work. He’s there with a man he barely knows, yet who’s his best friend, and he falls in love for the first time. And then there’s a murder, and he becomes a patsy. And he has to dig his way out figuratively while still working the only job that would have him.

There’s something powerfully understated about this one of Thompson’s. It’s largely a portrait; the crime story is almost incidental (it doesn’t even really appear until after the halfway point). Events are filed out matter-of-factly; death is common, brutal work is everyday. Tommy gets so badly sunburned he can’t move his face, but it’s just another day on the job.

But that reportage is what gives the book its power. As you read between the lines, you see that these men are still that, men. They’re men who have fallen between the cracks, whether it’s due to alcoholism or a bad night or just plain never having any opportunity in the first place. It’s Thompson casting a warm eye on them, showing them to be people with foibles and loyalty and complexity, when they’re commonly thought of as lazy and unskilled. Great stuff, well worth your time.
