Rating: 3/5

Think Graeber makes the point in his dawn of everything that you can trace all radical social critique back to reports written by Europeans on first encountering the relatively egalitarian societies they then set about destroying (Montaigne might be where they got this from). Ginzburg has a twist here whereby his miller comes to class consciousness - a more materialist Anabaptism as he puts it - from the proto science-fictional sections of Mandeville.
Its like a humanist variant of those generative anecdotes Foucault has, celebrating his mistakes and contradictions as indicative of an independent and roving intellect. As in Foucault there's a lot of stretches, but if you're of the view that the 16th c. is when you have the first coming together of the beginning of everything you need to get to capitalism as totality (transcendent ideology, state authority, colonies, trade, commercialised agriculture) but trying to square the seeming absence of an oppositional pole that is articulated (as opposed to perennial peasant uprisings), here is one.
