Rating: 4/5

It is such an old, old feeling, this unwillingness to comprehend that there can be death any where upon an earth that is so beautiful.

I picked this one up because I loved Lady Audley's Secret when I read it for a Victorian lit class in college, and I was just in the mood for something similar. 

Naturally, I was swiftly disabused of the notion that this book was like Mary Elizabeth Braddon's sensation novels in the Introduction, to the point where it made me nervous if I would like this one or not. Especially because apparently this is a rewriting of Madame Bovary, which I've never read and know nothing about, so I truly wasn't sure what I was going to think.

And I won't lie! It does have a slow start. We don't even meet the titular doctor's wife until chapter three, and the story itself didn't get juicy (jaw drops and side eyes and how are they possibly going to resolve this) until the last 150 pages. 

However! The reading experience is what really got me. I pulled out my bag of highlighters (my good Sharpie highlighters, the ones I haven't touched since 2023, which were zipped up in a little pencil pouch with a zipper that didn't move as quickly as it used to) because there were so many good lines, I couldn't not highlight them. I'm going to try and include as many of them in this review as I can, just to see if I can. 

The first hint of good luck that I had while reading was that there were some moments that were just funny to me. I wasn't sure if Braddon intended to be funny—I mean, I do know that when she wrote that Isabel thought of Roland (not her husband) while she was on her knees (praying at church), she did not intend for me to laugh, but you bet I absolutely did. But when she wrote that "the next best thing you can do if you haven't got ideas of your own, is to steal other people's ideas in an impartial manner"? Or "'It will be all the same over again, except that it will be a great deal more tiresome this time'" (insert Ben Affleck cigarette meme)? Or "'When I marry, my wife must love the people I love,' said the surgeon, who entertained those superb theories upon the management of a wife which are peculiar to youthful bachelors"? I don't know, I thought there was a certain bite to our narrator, and she brought some levity to the story at strangely perfect times!

Something I found funny that the narrator did eventually say point-blank near the end of the book was how obsessed Isabel Gilbert was with death. (This becomes incredibly ironic once the novel is over.) There are multiple times throughout the narrative before then, however, where Isabel is like "so what if I just died right now, I've always wanted to die young"—and this girl genuinely is not tortured at all (outside of the usual Victorian constraints on women's freedom, etc.) so what does she mean! My favorite such instance is when the man she's having an emotional affair with returns to town, seemingly to marry Lady Gwendoline Pomphrey, and Isabel muses that "there would be a fine wedding in the bright May weather. There was just time to go into a consumption between March and May, Mrs Gilbert thought, and her tombstone might be ready for the occasion, if the gods who bestow upon their special favorites the boon of an early death would only be kind to her." I'm sorry!! How am I not supposed to laugh at this!!

And I know I found this so funny, mostly because it was wild that the Victorians (the British in general probably) really did that: they stayed stuck in loveless marriages and they stayed unhappy because of culture/society/etc. (this isn't a paper for class so I'm substituting vibes right here instead of historical sources that explicitly state what I mean, because I know if you're reading this, you get the gist). Isabel would rather be "a very good wife, very gentle and obedient" and "furnish the secret chambers of her mind according to her own pleasure" instead of finding a way out of the marriage (if such a way existed) and making her dreams (minus the dying young, hopefully?) a reality. Instead of just separating and leaving people that didn't serve them, they stayed stuck, and they tortured themselves, coming up with elaborate metaphors to describe the pain of their lives. 

The problem is, if they weren't so eloquent about it, I'd be so mad at them! 

One of my favorite extended metaphors was the way Braddon wrote about Isabel being separated from either George or Roland by vast distances. With Gilbert, it was "together day by day, they live as much apart as if an ocean rolled between them; united by a hundred bonds, they want the subtle link that would have made them one; and, at the best, are only two separate creatures chained together." Something about that image was so evocative to me, I think because Isabel as a character really is in a vast ocean surrounded by no one and nothing, her only tenuous hold on reality her connection to George as his wife. Versus how with Roland, Braddon describes her clasping hands with him as "how closely they seemed linked together now; they who so soon were to be for ever parted; they between whom all the expanse of the Atlantic would have been only too narrow a barrier!" And later, them holding hands is described as "pressing [her hand] ever so gently,—only holding it, as if in that frail tenure he held the last link that bound him to love and life." The fact that George and Isabel are not well suited for each other is made very apparent early on, when the narrator tells us that "he sold his imagination, and Isabel lived upon hers." Irreconcilable differences is what would be on their divorce papers, that's all I'm saying! 

And so while I'm still laughing and thinking about how Isabel and George can just get a divorce so her and Roland can be together already, that's when Braddon hits me with this absolute sucker punch: "Would he be there? Her heart was perpetually beating out the four syllables of that simple sentence: Would he be there?" It really made me pause.

There had been sentences like that in the book before this moment, notably, "[Isabel] was so eager to be something. . . . Miss Sleaford wanted to be famous. She wanted the drama of her life to begin, and the hero to appear." Sentences that made me go yeah I get that, ones that were weirdly relatable, despite how this was published in 1864. But something about this one (Would he be there?) felt different to me.

Braddon then followed up with some absolute bangers: 

- Her hand did not tremble as it rested in his; but something like a mysterious winged creature that had long been imprisoned in her breast, seemed to break its bonds all at once, and float away from her towards him. She thought it was her long-imprisoned soul perhaps, that so left her to become a part of his.
- 'My darling, my darling! if fate had sent me here a twelve-month earlier, you and I might have been standing side by side in the moonlight, talking of the happy future before us. Only a year! and there were so many accidents that might have caused my return. Only one year! and in that little space I lost my one grand chance of happiness.' 
- It was only a dream—nothing more than a dream—that was lost to her.
- 'I went away, fully resolved never to look upon her face again; and then, and then only, I knew how truly and how dearly I loved her. I went from place to place; but I could no more fly from her image than from my own soul.'
- 'I recognise only the immortal right of two free souls, who know that they have been created for each other.' 
- She was no longer to enjoy that mystical double existence, those delicious glimpses of dreamland, which made up for all the dulness of the common world that surrounded her.
- 'It seems a grand story in that book, Izzie, but it's the commonest story in all the world.'
- 'Your soul has flown to me like a bird out of a cage; it is mine henceforth and for ever; as surely as that I love you,—fatally, unaccountably, mysteriously, but eternally. 

This part of my book is positively saturated with purple Sharpie highlighter ink. Something really struck me during this moment in the narrative, about how we as a species have not really experienced a singular original emotion in a very long time. Everything that we are feeling, someone else throughout time, in history, has felt. It was so validating that, at one moment, the narrator recognizes that feeling at one point, describing it as "an old, old feeling" (in relation to a specific thought about death and life, but a sentiment that I thought could have applied to the whole book)—and now made nearly 200 years older by the fact that the story still resonates today. Something about that made me feel strangely connected to these characters, and to all of humankind across time, in a way that nearly bowled me over.

I think, in this way, this book was one of the most humbling books I've ever read.

(Still not sure how I feel about that ending though, hence the 4-star.)
