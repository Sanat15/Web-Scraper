Rating: 5/5

Since I highly enjoyed Zoé Oldenbourg’s novel The World is Not Enough, I decided to pick up her scholarly account of the Albigensian crusade, a piece of history that I’ve always been fascinated with. It took me a while to get through the book due to the dense writing style.

Oldenbourg was able to give a comprehensive overview of the ideological pretext (of eliminating heresy) for the Albigensian crusade from the medieval church hierarchy, and also how it provided an opportune moment for the French lords to pursue a land grab of the wealthy Languedoc region. It was fascinating to know how the brutal campaign had little to do with religious fervour but was thoroughly driven by self-interest on the part of figures such as Simon de Montfort. Oldenbourg painted the French conquerors as barbaric actors, such that even the Catholic King of Aragon was compelled to join the defense of Languedoc alongside Count Raymond of Toulouse to ward off the northern threat. Despite successes in pushing out the French occupiers, the crusades eventually culminated in the bloody massacre at Montségur and led to the demise of Languedoc’s sovereignty as a distinct political entity.

Towards the end of the book, Zoé Oldenbourg highlighted the significance of the crusades in redefining the political landscape of southern France. I visited the beautiful city of Toulouse a while ago without knowing much of this period of history, but reading Zoé Oldenbourg has put that trip into perspective and helped me understand the weight of what happened in shaping the region since then.
