Rating: 4/5

There’s a certain exhilarating feeling when one sees what, in retrospect, is acknowledged as the enduring critical opinion on a work of art being formed for the first time. It’s the joy of witnessing an act of creation that shows us just how remarkable, at the very top of the profession, a critic’s perceptions can be, how heightened his intuitions. Wilson was a contemporary of Proust’s, Joyce’s, Elliot’s, Yeats’, Gertrude Stein’s, and Paul Valery’s - only a generation or so removed from Mallarme and Villiers de l’isle Adam. Reading this book, it is hard to believe that: not only from the voice, but also from the historiography and propulsion of the symbolist movement, the placing of the symbolists in context, the naturalists and romantics in their past and the modernists in their future. What was then a prediction - what these writers would come to mean - and what they have in fact come to mean, something that is now taken as a boring truism - has no distance at all. Posterity has proven Wilson. I’m very excited to read “To the Finland Station”, especially knowing that in that case Wilson himself acknowledged that he didn’t see Stalin’s gulags coming.
