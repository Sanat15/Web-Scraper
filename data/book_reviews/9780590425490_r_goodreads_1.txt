Rating: 5/5

There’s a certain magic to the way the past lingers in the air like the smell of fresh-cut grass on a summer’s day, or the unmistakable echo of a bat connecting with a pitch just right. You know the sound. The one that comes from the sweet spot. You don’t forget it, no matter how many years go by. And so it was, sitting in my old-man recliner, caught up and charmed in “Frank and Ernest Play Ball”, and finding in a children’s book more heart than I expected. But this isn’t the sort of book that wears its wisdom like an old man’s battered baseball cap. If you’ve been around long enough to have seen your fair share of dusty ballparks and know the taste of a ball park hot dog washed down with a cold root beer during a lazy summer game, there’s something about it that’ll take you back to a better time and place.
Here we’ve got an elephant and a bear--two mismatched friends who’ve got more grit than knowledge, though in their case, that's saying something. They’re hired hands, ready to take on any job that’s too big or too outlandish for the average folk. In this case, they’re tasked with managing a baseball team for a man named Mr. Palmer, a fellow who wants to whisk his wife away for their anniversary. Sounds simple enough, but there’s a catch--Frank and Ernest don’t know a thing about baseball.
What they do have, though, is a kind of dogged determination, the kind you see in the old-timers who’ve weathered too many summers to care about anything but giving their best. And so, through trial and error, through study and hard work, these two lovable characters set about learning the ropes of America’s pastime. 
The book’s a primer on baseball, yes, but it’s also a kind of love letter to the game’s history--one that never truly leaves you, even if you’ve spent the last few decades away from the ballpark. Day dedicates the book to legends of baseball’s glorious past, identified only by their famous nicknames. A few of those mentioned are “The Big Train”, “The Iron Horse”, “The Yankee Clipper”, “Dizzy”, and “Daffy”. 
And as for the finer details, the book’s filled with nostalgic touches that really made my day. “Take Me Out to the Ballgame”, “The Star-Spangled Banner”--the songs you could almost hear in the background, the smell of popcorn hanging in the air. Then there are the glossary pages too, a treasure trove of baseball terms that have grown scarce in the days of instant replay and tech-driven stats. I hadn’t seen some of those words in years, and reading them again was like opening an old box of mementos from a season long past.
The illustrations are vivid and full of life. They catch the rhythm of the game just as much as the words, and they show Frank and Ernest stumbling through their lessons with the sort of heart that makes you want to reach out and give them a pat on the back. You can almost hear the crack of the bat with each page and feel the dust rising from the infield as the players field grounders.
It’s clear that Alexandra Day, the author and artist, poured her heart into this one. She’s captured the spirit of baseball and wrapped it in a package that’ll delight fans of all ages. Whether you’re a kid learning the game for the first time, or an old-timer like me, still remembering the way the crowd would chant in the bottom of the ninth, there’s something timeless here.
Baseball, after all, isn’t just a game. It’s a place where friendship, hard work, and a little bit of grace can take you farther than you ever imagined. Just ask Frank and Ernest. They’ll tell you.
