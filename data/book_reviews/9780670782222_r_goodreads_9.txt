Rating: 3/5

I realize this children’s classic, Madeline, was first published in 1939—but it took reading “This Book Made Me Think of You” by Libby Page to finally bring me to it.

And, I’m surprised to find myself more unsettled than charmed. I also realize that there are several more Madeline stories that follow, but I don’t see myself checking them out from my local library.
 
Back to this one: I had intended to write a different review, but upon further reflection, I found the story feeling far less appealing than my initial reaction to it. 

Yes, it’s a classic. Madeline herself stands apart—fearless, spirited, and delightfully bold. The structure is neat, the rhyme memorable, and the setting iconic (a Paris boarding school with 12 girls overseen by the headmistress, Miss Clavel) —but the emotional core feels distant, even cold. The boarding school environment comes across as rigid and dim, more about order and routine than comfort or connection. 

That’s what makes the hospital scene stand out so starkly. When Madeline falls ill and has her appendix removed, she is suddenly surrounded by care, attention, and gifts. It’s the one place in the story where affection feels visible, even abundant. And yet, that warmth is fleeting. 

The girls’ fascination with her experience—almost envying the attention tied to pain—lands in an uncomfortable space, for this reader. It quietly raises the questions: why does care only appear in moments of crisis; and, what kind of environment makes illness seem desirable?

The ending doesn’t resolve that tension so much as leave it hanging. Instead of reassurance, there’s a lingering awkwardness—a sense that something emotional has been introduced but not fully explored.

Knowing a bit about Ludwig Bemelmans adds context. His difficult early life and exposure to upheaval seem to echo faintly beneath the surface. The darkness isn’t overt, but it’s there—in the restraint, in the emotional distance, in what’s left unsaid.

Interestingly, the true warmth of the book seems to live less in the story and more in the artwork. The Parisian settings—those glimpses of the Eiffel Tower, the Opéra, the city streets—feel like a genuine love letter, not through narrative, but through line and color. The illustrations carry a liveliness and affection (especially the colors) that the story itself withholds.

In the end, I can appreciate Madeline as a classic piece of children’s literature, but I found myself more detached than delighted – an experience that was thoughtful, but also quietly somber and uncomfortable. 

Of course, I’m reading this through an adult lens, which likely shapes—and perhaps limits—my experience of this classic. That may very well make me more of an outlier than the norm. I encourage you to read other reviews and see how it resonates for you.
