Rating: 4/5

"As dawn speared across the far west Texas prairie, the last of the night's heavy dew fell. I sat up shivering, looking down along the twisting bed of the dried-up creek where six hundred of us were jungled up while he waited for the pipeline job to start. The line was to be one the biggest jobs in years - all the way from here in this lonely gas field to Port Arthur on the Gulf." -- the opening lines

The crime-laced fiction work of Jim Thompson has been sort of hit-or-miss for me thus far. While I didn't particularly warm to The Killer Inside Me or The Getaway (although its film adaptation w/ Steve McQueen was a minor 1970's cult classic), his other books such as After Dark, My Sweet and The Grifters - both of which feature duplicitous dames and twisty hard-boiled plots - were more to my liking. Oddly, his South of Heaven is not quite like the latter two, but yet is nearly as good because of how Thompson smartly captures its characters amidst a time / place / era. The tale is narrated by the 21 year-old Tommy Burwell, a drifting orphan who makes a living as an itinerant roustabout or roughneck on the rural Texas plains of the 1920's. Though fairly young, Tommy is an experienced hard worker and even well-read (he composes limerick-like poetry) if not always life savvy. Author Thompson effortlessly sets up this plausible atmosphere - it probably helped that he grew up the son of a sheriff in the Oklahoma territory during that same period, and had spent time working in the region's oil fields - and THEN drops a crime story angle into the narrative. Said slant involves a murder and the planned theft of a company payroll, but as other reviewers have noted said felonious activity is actually less interesting than the day-to-day detailing of the working conditions, and the various personalities on the job or in the nearby town, during the initial two-thirds of the book.
