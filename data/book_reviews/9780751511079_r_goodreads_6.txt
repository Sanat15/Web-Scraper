Rating: 5/5

I do very much and firmly believe that I can certainly claim and point out with I think more than adequate truth and personal confidence that the absolute best parts of Ellis Peters' utterly delightful Brother Cadfael series of Mediaeval mysteries are generally and usually both Peters' spot-on and historically accurate sense of historical time and geographic place (how she makes Mediaeval 12th century England come both realistically and imaginatively, spectacularly to life) and equally so how Ellis Peters also manages to present historical mysteries which might include mayhem, murder, revenge, nastiness and the like but consistently in a textual manner where albeit the stories are always more than sufficiently descriptive and detailed, Peters' Brother Cadfael stories are (and happily so for me personally) never overtly, exaggeratedly horribly or gratuitously violently depicted, presented and told (not to mention that often if not even generally so in the Brother Cadfael series, the mysteries, the necessary whodunnit aspects thereof in fact and appreciatively so seem to come a trifle secondary to the day to day happenings of Shrewsbury and Brother Cadfael's home abbey of Saint Peter and Paul, and which I for one absolutely and hugely appreciate and majorly enjoy, and furthermore, how the English history parts Ellis Peters provides in and with her Brother Cadfael mysteries, these are superbly authentic, these are realistic and as such solid historical fiction and not annoyingly fantastical). 

With the sixth Brother Cadfael novel, with her 1983 The Sanctuary Sparrow (and which is also one of the first series novels I ever read, in 1984, when I was eighteen years old and really into Mediaeval mysteries) Peters in my humble opinion and aside from superb sense of historical time and geographic place (see above) gets all of the elements for a good and also for a cozy mystery wonderfully and utterly right. Thus in The Sanctuary Sparrow, Ellis Peters with textual delightfulness features an accused of murder and of robbery for whom we are totally rooting and whom we from square one so to speak absolutely know and feel to be completely, wholly innocent as well (and that the murder of which Liliwin is accused is not even a murder anyhow since it turns out that patriarch and "victim" Walter Aurifaber is only injured and nor in fact deceased). There exist and is presented by Peters in The Sanctuary Sparrow an array of possible suspects surrounding and involved with the toxically dysfunctional household of Aurifaber, there is a sweet budding love story (between itinerant jongleur Liliwin and the maid Rannilt) that we absolutely do not want to see interrupted and destroyed by a death sentence. 

And equally as well as importantly so, The Sanctuary Sparrow features a villain who turns out to be someone whom Ellis Peters describes with much understanding as well as with total kindness and also depicts as someone having more than legitimate and justifiable family caused anger issues and whose violence (and also whose death) is actually and completely shown as being first and foremost the fault of their family, of their father and of their enabling and pathetic grandmother (and that the actually murder in The Sanctuary Sparrow, that Baldwin Peche's death, while bien sûr not excusable, is also something he totally brought onto himself, not to mention that I would indeed even be kind of cheering for the villain in The Sanctuary Sparrow if they had not tried to shove Liliwin and Rannilt into harm's way). But indeed, behind everything of course, Peters with and throughout The Sanctuary Sparrow depicts and textually celebrates the wise and not ever too annoying evangelical and harshly judgmental ecclesiastical spirituality of the abbey of Saint Peter and Paul in Shrewsbury (except for Prior Robert and Brother Jerome that is and of course) and the ever maturing and nurturing friendship between Brother Cadfael and Hugh Beringar (and yes, that the in-depth analysis and description of the legal and religious tradition of sanctuary in which grants a forty-day grace period of safety as long as the accused stays within the abbey grounds is also meticulously and engagingly described by Ellis Peters, this makes me totally smile and I love love love the educational information provided by Peters in The Sanctuary Sparrow and indeed in ALL of the Brother Cadfael novels).

Five stars for The Sanctuary Sparrow, and no, that there is in The Sanctuary Sparrow not really any textual mention of the Civil War between King Stephen and the Empress Maud is actually for me something entirely and massively positive. For yes, we still in The Sanctuary Sparrow more than well realise in the background so to speak that times are volatile etc. and that it (and appreciatively so) makes total sense for Ellis Peters to show and to tell in The Sanctuary Sparrow how daily life does go on, that family squabbles and dysfunction arise, that marriage contracts are forged, how murder and mayhem can and does occur, that not EVERYTHING has to and needs to revolve around the monarchy and around the two royal cousins duking it out so to speak. And just to say in closing that The Sanctuary Sparrow is actually not only one of my absolute favourite Brother Cadfael mysteries, it is also one of the very few series novels I have found very nicely done and conceptualised in the PBS miniseries (since the vast majority of the episodes just seem to annoy me, but that the episode for The Sanctuary Sparrow is absolutely superb and also totally and successfully reflects as well as celebrates Ellis Peters' text).
