Rating: 2/5

"What do you think spies are - priests, saints or martyrs? They're a squalid procession of vain fools, traitors too, yes; pansies, sadists and drunkards, people who play 'cowboys and Indians' to brighten their rotten lives. Do you think they sit like monks, balancing the rights and wrongs?" -- bellicose protagonist Alec Leamas, a British intelligence operative, on page 214

I've read that author le Carre had intentionally structured his spy-centered books from the early 60's in direct opposition to (or, with his extreme dislike of) 'Bondmania,' as secret agent 007 was a steady presence on international bestseller lists and making inroads in the film world. Well, after reading The Spy Who Came in From the Cold, I think I'll stick with Ian Fleming's work - other than admittedly suspenseful first and final chapters, I found this slow-moving and just too talky. I'm also not sure it translates well to a 21st century audience, given that this was first published when the Cold War was still active and very much a hot topic. (However, lots of GR friends as well as other readers have given it high ratings, so opinions will vary.) Much of the text consisted of mostly men - the handful of involved characters uniformly came off as stiff, often spouting flat or humorless dialogue - sitting in a room together, conversing about persons / events that weren't particularly explained very well, making it like the reader was listening in on two complete strangers during a chat. It was boring in many stretches (and I get it - le Carre was going for some bitter realism here), and for a story only 200+ pages in length there were chunks of time I simply had no idea what the hell was going on. I'm glad other readers liked it a lot, but I was just puzzled by the fanfare attached to this book.
