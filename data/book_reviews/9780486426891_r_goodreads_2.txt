Rating: 3/5

When madness starts talking about humans, it has a lot to say. No one is spared, not even those who believe themselves to be the most learned, those theologians of yesteryear who held the unique interpretation of divine truth. Or these all-around specialists of today who bathe us with their empty speeches. Therefore, what is attractive in this little book, which tries not to take itself too seriously, is the portrayal of society's faults, particularly those of the Renaissance, in crisis and transition, much like our own. If very often the points are blunted by their targets' disappearance, we dream of a new praise of madness, which would show the ridiculousness of our time, but the book would undoubtedly take on far too essential dimensions. Madness is human; it has always been characteristic of man, and it gives life to existence by embellishing it, hiding shit under the carpet, and giving importance to what is not. Not but which encumbers our daily life. Madness is more necessary than wisdom, says Erasmus. So let's be crazy and believe it.
