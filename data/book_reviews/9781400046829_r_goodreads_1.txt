Rating: 4/5

Let us now praise the late Jimmy Breslin, a man who seemed incapable of writing dully. The Times in its obituary today called this "perhaps the quintessential Breslin" book, but I find it something of an outlier, although it demonstrates many of his virtues, the prose that is breezy but not without substance, dense with information without sinking of its own weight. And it has the trademark scornful wit aimed in the direction of politicians, in this case Rudy Giuliani and his too-close relationship with some shady builders associated with the Hasidim, dedicated voters. Breslin may have been the only writer in history who could make a narrative involving building inspectors and their reports (and bureaucratic clashes) move swiftly. But in this narrative he reaches far beyond the New York City that so possessed him to a small town in the Mexican countryside, the home village of the titular character. Despite straying so far from the five boroughs, he (or, more likely his researchers) spent considerable time talking to and understanding the people who knew Eduardo Gutiérrez. In that sense, the book is a considerable achievement, despite the very occasional mistake (Gutiérrez came from Nahua territory, not the Maya region, which is much farther south, has a completely different language, and whose people have emigrated at much lower rates--at least those not the object of brutal attack by the Guatemalan government). But in a sense, those Mexican villagers, whether at home, undergoing the trauma and danger of emigration, or underpaid at dangerous jobs where they are afraid to complain, are quintessential Breslin characters--not the gifted talkers he loved so much (Gutiérrez himself was almost pathologically shy), but the underdogs who keep on, the people trying to make their way against terrible odds. Breslin's subject was usually New York, but his prose travelled more widely than a lot of writers who use a lot of different settings.
