Rating: 5/5

Read Jonathan Carroll’s novel The Land of Laughs and you will discover a brilliant bit of literary legerdemain. It begins as a character driven literary story about an odd relationship and a shared obsession. Thomas Abbey is a bored English teacher at a Connecticut prep school who lives uncomfortably in the shadow of his deceased movie star father. He meets Saxony Gardner at a used bookstore where they discover their shared passion for Marshall France, a famous children’s book author (reminiscent of Ronald Dahl) whose whimsical works helped them survive traumatized childhoods. Both are strongly drawn, flawed characters, and their unusual relationship has a feel of ominous whimsy about it that will absolutely draw you in. 

Everything slides sideways at the novel’s halfway point. Tom Abbey took a sabbatical to work on a biography of his hero, and he and Saxony relocate to the small Missouri town where Marshall France had lived and worked. Here they found the writer’s daughter who agrees to assist, and a Norman Rockwellesque small town that is so picture perfect it almost feels creepy. At this point the whimsy begins to curdle into something far more ominous and sinister. It is a gradual change, but slowly progresses to all out eldritch dread as the book transforms into a fascinatingly unique horror tale. 

This was one of Carroll’s earliest novels, and it still stands out as one of his best. Those familiar with his work will notice his trademark touches — obsessions with high end fountain pens and bull terriers, as well as his ability to warp reality into odd shapes that will send subtle chills through your system. Rereading this book after more than a decade, I found it even stronger than my first time through. If you have never read any of Jonathan Carroll’s strange and brilliantly unique work, The Land of Laughs is a perfect entry point.
