Rating: 2/5

Staying as close to the facts as possible, trying to separate myth from reality, this fictional account of events surrounding The Gunfight at the O.K. Corral read unevenly. At times this reader found the line between novel and history blurry, as was the line between outlaw and hero. No clearly defined heroes or bullies in this story. All the characters had enemies setting up an era of violence based on feuds. This reader found the most sympathetic and interesting characters to be the women involved with the Earps. 

Estleman's writing is very descriptive of the period, characters, and the town of Tombstone with wonderful phrasing, such as his introduction of Allie, Virgil's wife, " . . . and although she distrusted all of the Earp brothers except Virgil, whom she loved with the considerable energy of an Irishwoman raised in Nebraska . . . " and his description of a Tombstone funeral: "Proper funerals were rare entertainments in an area whose cemetery contained more nameless corpses than Gettysburg's." 

Estleman's writing of the gun fights includes an unflinching description of the aftermath. It's like reading a pathologist's report. You, the reader, know exactly what the weapons do to the victims.

If you are aware of the people involved in The Gunfight at the O.K. Corral, but not familiar with what happened to the individuals after the dust settled, read no more than the book's last chapter.
