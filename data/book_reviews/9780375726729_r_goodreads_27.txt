Rating: 4/5

Eric Ambler's Journey Into Fear is a classic, highly influential spy thriller. Set in Istanbul early in World War II, the book focuses on Graham, a British arms merchant who is ambushed at his hotel by German spies. The local police discover that the Nazis fear that Graham's efforts will help strengthen Turkey's navy, and convince the government to side with the Allies. This leads Graham into a Hitchcockian labyrinth involving Romanian assassins, genteel Germans, Turkish double agents and the obligatory femme fatales. It's easy to peg a novel like this as "cliched"; certainly you've seen the tropes repackaged by a million later authors, and even in his day Ambler faced competition from John Buchan, Graham Greene and others. But thrillers are often less about originality than craftsmanship, and Ambler is certainly an impressive writer: his characters have the sharp ambiguity of film noir protagonists, and he excels at creating an atmosphere of suspenseful menace, with every character untrustworthy and the protagonist forced to rely on his luck and wits. Graham is a wonderful protagonist, initially detached from the goings-on around him but forced into a position of heroism. The book is grounded but never as dreary as Greene's work, and mostly lacking the prejudices that mar some of Buchan's (if anything, Ambler's rogues gallery seems positively cosmopolitan). Well worth reading for thriller fans, and anyone needing a piece of fun escapism. Received two film adaptations, one in 1943 with Joseph Cotten and another in 1975 featuring campy villain turns from Donald Pleasence and Vincent Price.
