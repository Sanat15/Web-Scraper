Rating: 3/5

This Bildungsroman reads like a mash-up of Robinson Crusoe, Heart of Darkness, Lolita, and Little Big Man. Part of the experiment here is that Ghose asks the reader to ride along with a narrator, Gregorio, who oversees a plantation in 1650 colonized Brazil--and, to make the ride realistic, the narrator, a scheming, opportunistic, and randy member of the Portuguese upper crust, wholly exemplifies the unspoken values and assumptions that he presumably would have had about slavery, marriage, children, money, and sex. There's not a hint of irony from the author, nor does the narrator have any moments of insight that bring him closer to a twenty-first century consciousness. I take it that this is Ghose's aim: to avoid moralizing when the facts of the story speak for themselves. That the reader's relationship with the narrator is, to say the very least, odd, is also part of the author's aim. There are interesting conversations, both in the book and in a reader's reactions, about what defines a country, the nature of freedom, and the chase for happiness--but there are other moments (all the sex scenes) that are cringeworthy. It's also peppered with actual historical figures, so someone who knows the history of Brazil might respond to this differently than I, who know very little, did.
