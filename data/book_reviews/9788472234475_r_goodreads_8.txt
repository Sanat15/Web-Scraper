Rating: 4/5

And now I have to stop. Because every time I remember this, I have to cry a little by myself. I don't know why something that made me so happy then feels so sad now. Maybe that is the way it is with the best memories.

This is Winnie. She is being extorted to reveal all the secrets of her life to her daughter. Should she fail to comply, and in a timely manner, her best friend Helen will do the job for her. Such is the entry point into Tan's familiar terrain; the deepening of relationships between mothers and daughters through the revelation of a catastrophic past. Where The Joy Luck Club made the round of women at a weekly mahjong table, The Kitchen God's Wife narrows the field to one - Winnie, whose life began as the daughter of a "Double Second" wife to a wealthy Chinese businessman, but soon descended to an existence of imposition upon distant relatives of decidedly less importance. World War II swells to lift her adolescence into the realm of sacrifice and forced selection, creating movement and misery and character-defining lessons that resonate to this day.

By focusing on one woman, and one turbulent historical saga, Tan is able to hone her gift for depth and detail. Artistic muscles are being flexed here, and to fine effect. Birth is given to a canvas of players who come alive through both their actions and inactions, words and silences, sins and charitable instincts alike. It is a well-rounded story that humanizes in the most surprising ways. If I had one lament it would be that the daughter, Pearl, whom all of this drama is descending upon, is rarely allowed to leave her seat. Such sidelining may or may not have been necessary; it did rob her of the attention I felt she was owed.

Still, a substantial tale told by a very talented author.
