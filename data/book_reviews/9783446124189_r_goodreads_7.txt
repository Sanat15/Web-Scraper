Rating: 4/5

Review by Irwin Ashish Coelho

3.5/5 🌟 

The Polish author Isaac Bashevis Singer (1904 to 1991) won the 1978 Nobel Prize for Literature, a no mean feat.

The first story set the tone, captivated me and made me want to read further. In it, a middle-aged man, slated to deliver a lecture in Spain, makes his way there from New York by sea. Upon landing, he meets a young girl, Hanka (a Jew), who purports to be his distant cousin. She regales him with tales of how she escaped the concentration camps by hiding in the home of a Gentile (a non-Jewish person) and passing off as an Aryan. There is no neat ending to this story, though, and that is fine by me.

Another lovely story concerns an admirer who deeply admires an author. She sets up a meeting with him to talk to him. Once she enters his home, the author gets a phone call from her husband, who tells him that all is not well with her wife. To add to the mystery, the author also gets a call from this lady's mother-in-law who conveys the same thing using different words. The mystery/suspense deepens further when the lady has an epileptic fit and the author needs to call for help, immediately.

Overall, almost all of the stories are drenched in melancholia, but few have a redeeming quality about them, and a unique aspect about this book is that the author worked with several translators, not just one or two, to shape the stories, most of which revolve around Judaism, Jews and the culture and traditions of the Jewish people.
