Rating: 1/5

This guide, originally published by Lewis Maxwell in 1961, is a bare minimum list of plants that do well in Florida. That DOES NOT mean that you should plant it in your yard. You will need to read closely to decipher whether the plant is compatible in your neck-of-the-woods paying close attention to "Freezes at about". Also, pay close attention to the pruning because IMHO there are several plants that are invasive. 

Pictures are in black and white and not very clear and concise. If you have grown the plant or seen it at nurseries you shouldn't have a problem with identification. But if you are not familiar with the vine, shrub or tree you will need to do further investigation.

I am not sure about the newer editions but my copy, reprinted in 1979, is dated and in some cases harmful. The Melaleuca, also called the cajeput or bottle brush tree, has invaded the Everglades, crowding out other species. Many people are allergic to it and it can also be a fire hazard. It needs to be destroyed. Mondo grass can be a problem if not carefully contained. The Brazilian pepper tree, favored by several Florida butterflies and birds, is one of the most invasive plants in Florida. The seeds, which germinate in most ecosystems, are widely distributed by birds. It is now prohibited and should be destroyed. Floridians need to be aware that competition from exotic plants harm both the natives that grow here and also discourage wildlife.

I encourage readers to set this book aside. I was going to donate mine to the library book sale but I have changed my mind. I don't want it on my conscience that someone planted one of the invasive or exotic plants that harm our fragile ecosystem. A much-better reference for anyone is BRINGING NATURE HOME by Douglas Tallamy. Look for the list of plants that do well in the southeast at the back of the book. And, again, put in the time to research a plant before including it in your landscape. In the long-run you will be glad you did.
