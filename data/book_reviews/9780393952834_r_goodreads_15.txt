Rating: 4/5

Smollett's trick—routing the same events through five letter-writing correspondents, each warped by vanity or innocence—is genuinely funny. Matthew Bramble rages at everything as he and his family and servants travel through Bath, the North of England, and the Scottish Highlands; everyone around him cheerfully overlooks his complaints. When the innocent fool, Humphry Clinker, enters the scene, the book grows funnier as he accidentally humiliates the pompous; Dickens obviously owes Smollett a debt for this.

The book is uneven, though. The back half loses pace, the jokes thin, and the ending rescues itself mainly through improbable sentimentality. There's a missed opportunity too: when the party passes through Smollett's own home territory in the Vale of Leven, Bramble delivers a warm little tribute to the author by name that verges on metafiction (Smollett's characters are staying at his house)—and that's it. A braver, more Sternean move would have put Smollett in the scene and let Clinker humiliate him in some innocent way. The fool exposing the author himself would have been the best joke in the book.

Which raises the question I kept returning to: what did these two actually make of each other? Sterne called Smollett "Smelfungus" in A Sentimental Journey — a pointed mockery of Smollett's travel writing as relentless, joyless complaint. Smollett returned no compliments. 

The surgeon and the parson, working the same vein of English comedy at the same moment in history, apparently couldn't see past the rivalry. They should have sat by a fire (both seem to always feel the damp) and told each other 18th-century fart jokes. They'd have gotten on fine.
