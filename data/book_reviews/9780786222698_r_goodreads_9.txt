Rating: 4/5

I do very much and firmly believe that I can certainly claim and point out with I think more than adequate truth and personal confidence that the absolute best parts of Ellis Peters' utterly delightful Brother Cadfael series of Mediaeval mysteries are generally and usually both Peters' spot-on and historically accurate sense of historical time and geographic place (how she makes Mediaeval 12th century England come both realistically and imaginatively, spectacularly to life) and equally so how Ellis Peters also manages to present historical mysteries which might include mayhem, murder, revenge, nastiness and the like but consistently in a textual manner where albeit the stories are always more than sufficiently descriptive and detailed, Peters' Brother Cadfael stories are (and happily so for me personally) never overtly, exaggeratedly horribly or gratuitously violently depicted, presented and told (not to mention that often if not even generally so in the Brother Cadfael series, the mysteries, the necessary whodunnit aspects thereof in fact and appreciatively so seem to come a trifle secondary to the day to day happenings of Shrewsbury and Brother Cadfael's home abbey of Saint Peter and Paul, and which I for one absolutely and hugely appreciate and majorly enjoy, and furthermore, how the English history parts Ellis Peters provides in and with her Brother Cadfael mysteries, these are superbly authentic, these are realistic and as such solid historical fiction and not annoyingly fantastical). 

But furthermore, with the eleventh Brother Cadfael novel, with the published in 1985 An Excellent Mystery (which I originally read in 1988 and have just both reread and adored as much today, in 2026, as I did then), while yes, there definitely and like the book title points out is indeed a mystery, there is also not an actual murder to solve. Sure, when it becomes clear in An Excellent Mystery that Julian Cruce never arrived at her convent, Peters describes how there is the suspicion and the fear of an unsolved murder. But and to be perfectly honest here, even when I read An Excellent Mystery in 1988, I did manage to figure out long before Brother Cadfael, Brother Rhun, Hugh and Aline Beringar did what Brother Fidelis' secret is (and that there also was not ever a murder), how Julian Cruce transformed herself to be the mute (and by all appearances male) Brother Fidelis so that she could secretly become Brother Humilis' companion, since she considered herself to be Godfrid Marescot's wife even though he has become a monk, even though he has become Brother Humilis due to the grievous and lasting injuries he suffered in the Holy Land and thus had released his betrothed, but how Juian Cruce obviously does not want to be released, that for her she simply and absolutely is Godfrid's spouse and would even become a mute monk to secretly attend him and be his constant companion as Brother Fidelis. 

And with An Excellent Mystery being a sweet, albeit equally so rather a heartbreaking but also gloriously uplifting story of loving devotion and of Julian Cruce doing what she considers both her duty as well as her pleasure (that even though Godfrid Marescot has released Julian from the engagement formed when she was still a child and with him just about to leave on Crusade, she does not want this and she thus finds a way to secretly be the companion of Brother Humilis and just like a devoted and loving wife would be to her husband), a wonderful and poignant story, although I do think that the scenario with Brother Urien feel a bit artificial and tacked somewhat tacked on by Ellis Peters (and which is also why instead of five stars, my rating for An Excellent Mystery is but four stars, but still, An Excellent Mystery truly is a wonderful story and one that I have hugely enjoyed rereading and will more than likely reread in the future).
