Rating: 1/5

I received this book back in 2001 when I was just 8 years old and didn’t speak English. I was told to “make my own decisions” about the Harry Potter series, but when I tried reading it at that age, I found it too complicated and never picked it up again. Now, with some free time, I decided to give it another go—and I can confidently say it’s a mess.

Abanes seems to have written this book more for a quick profit than out of any serious biblical scholarship. His focus is only on the occult, and his attempts to integrate scripture into his critique fall flat. The whole thing feels rushed, like an attempt to capitalize on the hysteria surrounding the series at the time, with little to no research backing up his claims.

The book opens with a foreword by Doug Groothuis, who criticizes the Harry Potter series for featuring morally flawed, egocentric characters who lie, practice occultism, use profanity, and refuse to repent. This makes me wonder—if Groothuis doesn’t like morally flawed characters, how does he view King David or Paul? Both were deeply flawed but are key figures in the Bible. Are they somehow less problematic?

One of the strongest points Abanes attempts to make is that the Harry Potter characters are morally imperfect, making mistakes and even engaging in behaviors like lying or cheating. But isn’t that precisely what makes these characters relatable and human? As one of my seminary professors would say, “Pagans gonna pagan.” Harry Potter resonates with readers because of the imperfect characters.

Another issue with Abanes’ book is that he jumped on the Harry Potter critique bandwagon before the series was finished, missing the larger picture. By only critiquing the first few books, he gives an incomplete and one-dimensional view of the story as a whole. It feels like nothing more than a cash grab during a time when parents were panicking about the books.

I also can’t help but wonder how Abanes would respond if he had read the entire series instead of just the first few books. Would he still ignore the clear parallels between Harry and Christ? Would he overlook the profound Christian themes woven throughout the books—themes of sacrifice, redemption, and the battle between good and evil? Would he dismiss the fact that J.K. Rowling, herself a Christian, has openly admitted that her faith influenced the story, even going so far as to say she kept it quiet for fear that readers would guess the ending? It seems like an essential part of the conversation that Abanes either missed or chose to ignore in his rush to critique.

Abanes also contradicts himself several times. Early on, he claims that the Philosopher’s Stone has a real-life counterpart, only to later say the book’s version of the stone doesn’t resemble it at all. He also states that the spells used in the Harry Potter books don’t have any real connection to witchcraft, which completely undermines his earlier arguments.

If you’re a millennial and this book is influencing your opinion of the Harry Potter series, I’m sorry. There’s really no value in this book—it doesn’t provide an informed critique, and it doesn’t do justice to the broader conversation around the series.
