Rating: 4/5

I found this novel to be terrifying, and almost prescient, in light of current news in the United States (I write this review in July 2013).

Abigail Goodman makes a private medical decision, and is on trial for her life because of it. In this novel, abortion is a crime, punishable by death, and she has had one. Not only is the subject of Choice up for discussion in Howard Fast's story, but also the continuing chipping away at the autonomy of women. I would normally cringe when a man sets out to write a book so incredibly pertinent to a woman's experience, since my usual stance is that if you don't have a uterus, you can't possibly know what it's like to attend to the issues that go along with that. However, Mr. Fast pretty much nailed my experience down, so either he was a very observant man, or he had women in his life who told him what they were dealing with. 

As a legal procedural, this was fascinating to read and fairly accurate to my experience of how US court procedures work (with literary license, of course - if you ever sat down to read how a trial *really* goes, you'd be asleep in ten minutes). The cynic (and observer) in me doesn't believe that a trial would be conducted so fairly or objectively, but the reactions of the press, put throughout the novel as filings from various sources, are spot on.

The story depicted here is something that sounds far-fetched, but could happen. All that would be needed would be a slightly scarier Supreme Court and more governors such as those who already exist in certain states (including the one in which I was born, I'm sad to say). I only wish I could have read it and said "that would never happen." Instead, I said "he wrote this in 1993, and in 2013 there are people who are working to make this non-fiction."
