Rating: 3/5

I'm not a huge fan of Westerns, but I am from Arizona--and in fact, live in southeast Arizona just a couple of hours away from Tombstone--and am interested in Arizona history. So when I saw a paperback copy of Who Rides with Wyatt, a novelization of Wyatt Earp's career as a lawman in Tombstone, in a local thrift store, I had to buy it and eagerly looked forward to reading it. My anticipation was also whetted by my experience with the only other Will Henry book I've ever read: Reckoning at Yankee Flat, a historical novel about highwayman Henry Plummer and his activity during the Montana gold rush, which I read back when I was a junior in high school for an American History book report assignment. I thoroughly enjoyed that book, and apparently it was historically accurate enough for my American history teacher to accept it as a fulfillment of the assignment to read a work of nonfictional history. 

Unfortunately, Who Rides with Wyatt is definitely not historically accurate, as far as current research into the lives of Wyatt Earp, Doc Holliday, Johnny Ringo, and the town of Tombstone, Arizona currently reveals. This book was first published in 1955, well before definitive histories of Wyatt Earp, Doc Holliday, et. al., were written. I don't know if Will Henry wrote his story based on the best guesses and most accurate information extent at that time, or if he deliberately made up certain details (such as the close, almost parental relationship between Wyatt Earp and Johnny Ringo), just to make the story more dramatic (after all, Henry Wilson Allen, a.k.a. "Will Henry," was a successful screenwriter), but I've read Casey Tefertiller's 1997 Wyatt Earp: The Life Behind the Legend, so all the specious details were distracting and even annoying.
