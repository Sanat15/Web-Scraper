Rating: 4/5

Damn, it's hard to review Kallocain. Karin Boye published it in 1940; a few months later, she killed herself. The book is one of the bleakest, most heartrending things I have ever read. It anticipates 1984, which wouldn't be written for another decade, and goes further. Orwell's book ends with Winston Smith's realisation that he loves Big Brother. Leo Kall (kall is "cold" in Swedish) the narrator of Boye's novel, takes it for granted that everyone must love Big Brother except some human scum who should be identified and eliminated as quickly as possible. He's come up with an invention that he's sure is going to help. Kallocain is a truth serum that's one hundred percent effective. You're given a dose, and immediately you lose all your inhibitions and say what you're really thinking. Kall figures it will be the perfect way to unmask those spies and traitors who have so far managed to hide.

Boye is a poet with the poet's acute sense for the beauty of language, but here she goes to great lengths to hide that beauty and only let it shine through rarely and indirectly. Everything in Kall's world is ugly. His society is ugly, his life is ugly, the stiff, formal words he uses to tell his story are almost always ugly. Occasionally something happens which shows him the possibility of another existence, but throughout most of the novel he does his best to ignore these annoying distractions and focus on what's truly important: perpetuating the World State and its ability to control its citizens. Of course, the system is insane: as soon as Kall's invention starts being used on a large scale, it becomes clear that almost everyone is harbouring treasonous thoughts. Logically, Kallocain is a reductio ad absurdum of the whole enterprise, but people are not logical. It doesn't make much difference that everyone knows their society is built on a lie.

Probably not a good idea to think too much about this book.
