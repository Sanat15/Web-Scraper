Rating: 5/5

Before the old legends die they perform a public service. Eric Hobsbawn looks at the Sicilian mafia, Brazilian cangaceiros (rural bandit kings) and "Robin Hood" outlaws like Jesse James, at least the Henry Fonda version from 1939, and Salvatore Giuliano of post-war Italy as personifying a crude but effective response to the encroachment on their territory by capitalism, the modern bureaucratic state and that ultimate symbol of both, the railroad. In that sense they are primitive, or premodern. These last holdouts from modernity are "social rebels", usually bandits, sometimes unwittingly at other times consciously, defending the folk around them from an oppressive new order signified by tolls, taxes, transportation and rural policemen. Social rebels are not revolutionaries. That category must be reserved for the likes of Pancho Villa, Emiliano Zapata or the Mau Mau of Kenya, who fought under a political banner and a program, no matter how inchoate. Primitive rebels are doomed by the forces of history but Hobsbawn champions them in offering another path than that of obligation to the state.
