I must have read this book before, but for the life of me I can't remember it. It's set in the time of Charles II of England, and has a very long subtitle. Otherwise, it seems a fairly realistic view of Restoration England, then Bermuda (w/a sidelong glimpse of the Sargasso Sea), then Colonial Maryland. 

Aside from the travelogue aspects, there's little substance. It seems to be a chapter from the gospel of hard work, and in several cases it's clear that the hard work is not only unneccessary, but destructive. Why, for example, did they not adopt the three sisters model of horticulture, which would have been much less destructive (and less work)? Granted, the farming that's done is less destructive than tobacco--but that's not saying much--most things are. And why hack out the brush along the river? Not only does the brush protect from flooding, erosion, etc, it also forms nursery habitat for fish, etc in the rootage pools. And why, when becalmed at sea, did nobody even consider plankton as a food source? Darwin's accounts imply that the plankton levels were high even as late as the 1830s. Must be better than rats, eh?

Norton was always uneasy on the issue of social inequalities. She tried to argue both ways--that a kind master is better than harsh freeholding, and that social inequities should be eased, if only by a little. This book contains both arguments, parsed hardly at all.
