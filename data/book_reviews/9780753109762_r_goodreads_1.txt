Rating: 4/5

After reading Eight Perfect Murders and rewatching Alfred Hitchcock’s classic Strangers on a Train, along with my favorite comedy adaptation, Throw Momma from the Train (Danny DeVito was absolutely brilliant—if you haven’t seen it, I highly recommend it!), I finally decided it was time to read the original novel by Patricia Highsmith that inspired these unforgettable films.

Overall, the central premise is chilling and fascinating: the idea that every ordinary person may carry the capacity to kill, that one dark suggestion at the wrong moment could place evil in the driver’s seat and send someone spiraling down their darkest internal highways. It’s a provocative, disturbing, and undeniably brilliant concept.

That said, I have to admit that the screenplay adaptations made some necessary and incredibly effective changes. The films added sharper characterization, clearer motivations, and more immediate emotional stakes. In comparison, the novel leans heavily into long, philosophical passages examining the violent and untamed nature of human beings. While intellectually interesting, these sections sometimes lessened the tension and slowed the pacing. A few chapters and extended internal monologues could have been trimmed to preserve the story’s thriller momentum.

Still, this novel remains a remarkable achievement. It’s impossible not to admire how one psychological thriller sparked so many creative reinterpretations and inspired legendary filmmakers to create masterpieces from its bones. Reading it now, especially after revisiting those films, felt like participating in a living conversation between literature and cinema.

I’m confidently giving it four stars—and now I’m excited to revisit Rebecca next. I’ve dedicated the past two weeks to rewatching Hitchcock’s films and reading the novels that helped shape his iconic vision, and it has been an absolutely thrilling, immersive journey into the mind of suspense itself
