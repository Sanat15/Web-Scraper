Rating: 3/5

I avoid reviews, unless I am undecided about a book. Then they do me a service. If a novel is disliked, acquainting myself with the criticized aspects generally neutralizes them and I enjoy the story better. I have read Charlotte Armstrong over the years and like a mixed box of chocolates, the quality is all over the spectrum. Stephen King is respected for originality but “Misery”, 1987, resembles “The Witch’s House”, 1963, a little bit. It has a wholly different premise but includes an imprisoned, injured man. There are tense moments for her indisposed character but his wife and the daughter of another professor, were more distressed while the men were missing.

One attribute that must be given credit, for the first time, is the high quality of the language. Charlotte was one of the exceptional authoresses. Unfortunately, the excellence of a story relies on the structure, focus, and pace. Anyone seeking a witch story should know that it is nothing but a derisive nickname for a disturbed woman of a shabby house. Hiding a man was slightly plausible because only a grocery deliverer had contact with her. Mystery lovers might find this format lacking because readers follow the professors; missing only from their family’s point of view. Urgency is generated by wishing relief for their wife and daughter. Readers also know that two personages hoped to prevent the reunion.

Few characters were likeable and a university dean was stupid. There is action but it derives from readers willing the ladies to search the right spots and external personages, to notice distress messages. The crooks were despicable and Charlotte did not shy away from violence. However, there was no firmly established protagonist and people in proximity to the hostage connected the dots too slowly. Three stars are an even grade.
