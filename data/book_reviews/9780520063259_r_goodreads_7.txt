Rating: 3/5

UPDATE (30/06): After my visit to Kenya and mingling with the tribe people, I'd say that many of the traditions have changed, yet it's still a reliable source for how things used to be in the past 

First, I picked this book for some cultural context on the Maasai Mara culture ahead of a Safari trip I'm prepping for. It serves its purpose as an introductory source on understanding how the dynamics of their culture work, their beliefs and lifestyles, but nothing deeper. The same level of knowledge on the tribe can be easily found online, like in Britannica or featured in National Geographic.

However, I believe where the real value of the book lies is as a case study of radical life transformation. 

Saitoti's personal story gives the book a far richer depth..

This autobiography is about one of the very first members of his tribe to enrol in a British missionary-founded school. Saitoti tells his story from the age of 6 to his thirties. A very unique life path! Not only as one of the first to receive education in a school, departing his tribe from a young age, but also for how this experience shaped his life afterwards. A lifestyle that shifted from a hunting-and-gathering to a technology-based modern one. A boy who could barely speak Swahili (which is the official language of his own country) to a man learning and lecturing in the US Ivy League. Has he been lucky? Of course! But it's not only about luck; he's a very determined person, with great self-discipline, whose life path is genuinely worth respecting.

What intrigued me most was the mindset transformation unfolding across the different stages of his life and the flexibility he showed, seizing the opportunities for his goals whenever and wherever possible. 

Although I enjoyed the 1st half more, which was about his life in the tribe, I appreciate the 2nd half. I'm not approaching the book as a literary work, but as a testimony of someone who had a unique life path, wrote a book about that life as a record, and shared it with the world to remind us of a dying culture and nearly disappearing tribe.

P.S National Geographic's Man of the Serengeti (1972) is inspired by Saitoti's story. I've watched it, and I'm hugely in favour of the book over the movie—it simply doesn't tell the whole story.
