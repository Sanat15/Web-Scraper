Rating: 5/5

Athena is my tenth John Banville novel, and the final installment of The Frames Trilogy. 
 
Freddie Montgomery has neatly established himself as a connoisseur and expert in the world of art dealing. Banville cultivates a distinctly noirish atmosphere, using it to tell a story of doomed romance, crime, art, destiny, and the strange, often inscrutable patterns that shape a person’s life. 
 
There’s a really interesting paradox at the center of the novel—that it’s in art, in myth, in fantasy, where we might find the truest reflection of the human soul. Or maybe not “true,” exactly, but something more revealing. Art becomes a kind of instruction manual for living, offering us experiences and emotional textures that reality itself can’t quite provide. And alongside that is the idea of self-invention, the ongoing act of curating an identity, of presenting the most compelling version of ourselves to the world. 
 
Structurally, the novel revolves around eight fictional 17th c. paintings that Freddie is hired to authenticate. Each chapter opens with a description of one of these works and a brief biography of its painter before segueing into Freddie’s own story. The connection between art and life is constant and deliberate, and Banville draws on the language of painterly technique and invokes a wide range of artistic and literary references as Freddie gradually sketches himself into being. 
 
I love the voice Banville finds for this novel. The way Freddie speaks directly to the reader, the doubling back, the self-corrections, the wry, bemused asides, the conversational ellipses as he searches for the right phrasing. This sense of watching thoughts formulate in real time  gives the prose a wonderful sense of immediacy and play. 
 
Banville’s style here is at its most digressive, lyrical, and unapologetically pretentious. I know enjoyment of this kind of writing is a matter of personal taste, but for me, it’s perfect and I loved every second of reading this.
