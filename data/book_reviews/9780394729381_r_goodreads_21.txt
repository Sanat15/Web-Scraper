Rating: 3/5

If you've never read John Gardner, this is not the place to start. Published just months before he died in a motorcycle accident, it's a dark, brooding tale. Mickelsson is a philosophy professor whose life is falling apart. He's in the middle of divorcing a money hungry wife, is practically bankrupt and has the IRS chasing him for back taxes. He's worried about his son who is involved in anti-nuclear activities and is currently missing. Socially awkward, Mickelsson has no close relationships with his faculty colleagues except for a charming Sociologist, and a paid relationship with a young woman from town. 

He has had a bout with mental illness earlier on before moving to this university and seems to be heading in that direction again. He has questions about everything: the various philosophical viewpoints he has famously held until now, his teaching, the nature of love and life. In the middle of his dark night of the soul, Mickelsson buys an old farmhouse and devotes his energies to renovating it. To some extent this helps his peace of mind but the haunted house begins to wear on him. The story becomes quite rambling as Gardner weaves the story of the ghosts that haunt the farmhouse (whom Mickelsson comes to see) with Mickelsson's personal ghosts (worries, regrets, failings, loneliness, etc), and bizarre stories of the area.

Readers who know a little of Gardner's history will recognize autobiographical elements such as the details of Mickellsson's youth on a farm, his knowledge of the Susquehanna area (Gardner lived there at the time), the philosophical and political viewpoints. Expect a lot of philosophy, a lot of poking fun at political figures like Reagan. 

While there are some brilliant passages of prose and some vivid descriptions, this reader often wondered if it was worth continuing to read and also came away with the distinct impression that Gardner was at the time of writing undergoing his own dark night of the soul.
