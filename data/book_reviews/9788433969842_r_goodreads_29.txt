Rating: 3/5

‘She said, ‘When I was in my twenties, living off the King’s Road and knowing fashionable people, I was quite a strange girl, lonely and…’ She searched for the word. ‘Extreme. I haven’t made the most of myself’’

The idea of making the most of oneself sits at the emotional core of Hanif Kureishi’s ‘Gabriel’s Gift’. Kureishi is often read as cynical or provocatively unsentimental, but ‘Gabriel’s Gift’ contains an uncharacteristic optimism that is rooted in creativity. Gabriel’s creativity is tentative, adolescent, and even delusion at times, yet its persistence suggests, perhaps naively, that self-possession enacts possibility.

It is perhaps unfair to say Gabriel’s Gift suffers in comparison with the novels that established his reputation, but against the cultural satire and political charge of ‘The Buddha of Suburbia’ or ‘The Black Album’, this novel can feel slighter: less funny, less daring in its social analysis, and less attuned to a historical moment. Its plot lacks the energy of those earlier works, but where Kureishi’s most celebrated novels look outward, this one turns inward to explore imagination, creativity, and the permission they extend.

What distinguishes the novel is its insistence that optimism can coexist with loss. Gabriel’s twin brother died young, but remains within Gabriel- less of a ghost and more of a function of creativity: the imagined other who authorises risk, whispers encouragement, who allows action when self-doubt or parental disappointment might otherwise paralyse. In this sense, creativity is not born of confidence but of division. And optimism is a fragile but necessary stance, an emotional posture that allows life to continue in the aftermath of grief: to imagine a life might amount to something is an act of courage.

Against the backdrop of unfulfilled potential- his mother never establishing a career in fashion, and his father, once a hugely talented bass player turned almost-homeless drunk mired in debt- Gabriel’s creativity can appear insolent. He grants himself permission to dream, and this idea of permission- who gives it and who withholds it- runs quietly through the novel.

In a turn that feels radical for Kureishi, the novel culminates undeniably positively. It ends in action, Gabriel beginning the production of his film. Kureishi also allows Gabriel’s father, a figure of squandered talent, to recover a sense of purpose in teaching music. He finds a modest but genuine optimism, one grounded not in fantasy but in transmission, the act of passing something on. The novel suggests fulfilment need not arrive in the form initially imagined, and making the most of oneself may involve revision rather than redemption.
