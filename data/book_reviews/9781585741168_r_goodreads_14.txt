Rating: 5/5

The Arctic Grail draws on a number of primary sources to create a narrative about its powerful main character: the impenetrable Arctic. With a focus on the dispositions of the great Arctic explorers, the societies that worshiped them, and the Inuit peoples that saved and assisted them, Bertin effectively conveys the supernatural allure of the Arctic.

The book is filled with compelling heroes: William Kennedy, the Canadian fur trader who led the first Arctic expedition that did not lose a single man, and the first to effectively incorporate Indigenous travel methods to better explore the Arctic; the Norwegian explorers Fridtjof Nansen, and Roald Amundsen, who achieved unprecedented success by spurning the expedition structures of the British Arctic Establishment; and Salomon August Andree, whose stoicism and attempt to reach the North Pole via hot air balloon is like something from a child's dream.

I enjoyed every second I spent reading this book. It is hard to imagine that a more comprehensive record currently exists on the topic. A must-read for those with a budding interest in Earth's polar regions.
