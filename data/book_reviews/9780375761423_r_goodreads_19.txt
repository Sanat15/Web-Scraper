Rating: 4/5

Published in 1988, Alaska is a sprawling historical fiction written in Michener's trademark style. It begins in geological deep time and sweeps forward through millennia to arrive at the present, using a rotating cast of individuals to tell the story of the forces shaping a place. At roughly 900 pages, it is not one to pick up lightly.

The opening chapters on tectonic collision, glaciation, and land bridge migrations, complete with a woolly mammoth named Matriarch, drag somewhat, but once humans arrive on the scene, the novel becomes fully engaging. The book moves through historical eras: the arrival and dispersal of Native peoples, Russian exploration and the brutal fur trade following Vitus Bering's 1741 expedition, the 1867 purchase by the United States, the Klondike and Nome gold rushes, the salmon canning industry, the push for statehood, and the pipeline era. Each section introduces new characters. We meet Russian Orthodox priests, Aleut hunters, American prospectors, Chinese cannery workers, bush pilots, Iñupiaq sea captains, and many more. 

The book's strength lies in its middle sections, particularly the gold rush chapters. The individuals introduced here, along with their descendants, become lasting characters through the rest of the book. Michener excels at explaining how things worked, such as salmon canneries, pipeline engineering, and the methods of survival in extreme conditions. He clearly researched Native Alaskan cultures extensively, and while the portrayals have dated somewhat, he treats Indigenous characters as full historical actors.

This is classic Michener with his conviction that one cannot understand a place without understanding how it was physically made. The contemporary sections lose steam considerably, but for readers who want breadth over depth and can appreciate Alaska as a place where climate and landscape dominate human life to an unusual degree, this book contains valuable information on how Alaska became what it is. It is an excellent example of ambitious, imperfect popular history in novel form.
