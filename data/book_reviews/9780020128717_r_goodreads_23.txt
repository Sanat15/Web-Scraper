Edmund Wilson’s excellent “Axel’s Castle” delivers what the subtitle promises, “A Study of the Imaginative Literature of 1870-1930,” starting with the Symbolists, whose sometimes murky modus operandi are explored with admirable clarity, and moving from there to Yeats and the arch-Modernist work of Eliot, Joyce, Proust, Stein, et al, concluding with a look at the antic Dadaist crew. His tremendous admiration for these great writers doesn’t preclude skepticism where warranted, and his warm praise is often balanced by lucid critiques; even the sainted Yeats, Proust, and Stein get balanced assessments, as does Joyce, although “Finnegans Wake” was still in progress when this book appeared in 1931. Wilson’s focus is limited to French and Anglophone authors, but within those parameters his analyses and opinions are just marvelous. His criticism is considerably more engaging than that of Leavis or even Eliot, and I regret that it’s taken me so long to read this volume cover to cover. Highest recommendation.
