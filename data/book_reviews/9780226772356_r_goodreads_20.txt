Rating: 3/5

I may ultimately change my mind about this one, but for the more moment I'm calling myself disappointed.

There were some extremely powerful passages, led by Lieber's recitation of horrors, Gideon's speech on the impossibility of true justice or retribution, and perhaps most notably (and most disturbingly) Steiner's imagined Hitlerian speech in self-defense, which made it well worth the read, and it's definitely Interesting. But Steiner invents a fascinating hypothetical, then declines to use it to tell a story and instead to pose a number of philosophical, moral, legal, political and practical questions that it would raise... which he doesn't get particularly deep into exploring. 

Perhaps merely looking at all the angles one could examine the problems raised by the hypothetical capture of Hitler from was satisfactory for the time of publication - the year Mengele drowned and two years after the first accusations were leveled at John Demjanjuk, a time when it seemed more plausible his audience or the society he was writing for would have to work out the answers. At this point in history, it's more than a little unsatisfying, especially because I would have been fascinated to read the conclusions of someone who was clearly extremely thoughtful, willing to embrace complexity and nuance and as concerned with such serious matters as Steiner. (Although it occurred to me in reading this is part of his point: that ultimately truly justice or fulfilling vengeance would be impossible and whatever resulted from this moment would be relentlessly twisted by national interests, international politics, personal desire to avoid responsibility or the broader questions a trial of Adolf Hitler might raise.

It ultimately felt incomplete to me... like the beginning of an epic rather than a complete novella in its own right.
