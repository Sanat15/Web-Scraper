Rating: 4/5

Genteel, sincere, well researched, and more than a little didactic -- this is historical romance in the mode of Johnny Tremain (complimentary). Jane Austen wrote of her contemporaries with arch sarcasm, Georgette Heyer wrote of her ancestors with shameless glee, but Chetwynd-Ley presents Georgian society as frivolous but ultimately moral. At least the rakish hero is secretly moral, and not in the modern approach of a troubled past justifying unvirtuous behavior, but of a bored man who simply hasn't met the right woman: smart, earnest, and uninterested in vanity. Chetwynd-Ley's style is flat and literal; the detailed inclusion of historical figures such as Hannah More and Fanny Burney gives the air of book report rather than of RPF. I didn't believe Sir James's sudden turn towards the serious and loyal but it wasn't clunky so much as improbable and not in the realm of the real. The whole thing felt like a cozy parable, yet somehow more compelling than a smut-less, banter-less, hijinks-free, unstylish romance should be. I enjoyed it.
