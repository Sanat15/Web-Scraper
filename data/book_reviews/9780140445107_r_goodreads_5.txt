Rating: 4/5

The Art of Rhetoric demonstrates the art of persuasion, all of its faculties, and how to use them appropriately. It is here that the rhetorical proofs ethos, pathos, logos, and sometimes kairos come from. Aristotle establishes the art of persuasion in his first book and identifies some its tools, such as the linguistic syllogism, the figurate metaphor, or even the importance of rhythm and meter in oratory rhetoric. What I enjoy most about the Art of Rhetoric is that it does not simply discuss rhetoric alone, but also focuses on when to properly use rhetoric and which what each type of rhetoric requires. Asking for a favor or second chance (deliberative rhetoric), defending yourself from an accusation or delivering one yourself (forensic rhetoric), or giving a special someone some well-deserved praise or a backhanded remark (display), all are rhetoric. Aristotle says that rhetoric is a part of our daily lives. I agree, we use it in our everyday discussions and conversations knowingly or unknowingly. The same can be said for when you are trying to impress or disinterest somebody. That's body rhetoric. 

The book even comes with lists of motives for various emotions, such as anger, jealousy, and their opposites (I find this to be its own extension from Aristotle's Poetics, which if that explained how to write a plot, the Art of Rhetoric explains how to write characters).

Highly enjoyable from one of the best to ever do it.
