Rating: 3/5

I do very much and firmly believe that I can certainly claim and point out with I think more than adequate truth and personal confidence that the absolute best parts of Ellis Peters' utterly delightful Brother Cadfael series of Mediaeval mysteries are generally and usually both Peters' spot-on and historically accurate sense of historical time and geographic place (how she makes Mediaeval 12th century England come both realistically and imaginatively, spectacularly to life) and equally so how Ellis Peters also manages to present historical mysteries which might include mayhem, murder, revenge, nastiness and the like but consistently in a textual manner where albeit the stories are always more than sufficiently descriptive and detailed, Peters' Brother Cadfael stories are (and happily so for me personally) never overtly, exaggeratedly horribly or gratuitously violently depicted, presented and told, not to mention that often if not even generally so in the Brother Cadfael series, the mysteries, the necessary whodunnit aspects thereof in fact and appreciatively so seem to come a trifle secondary to the day to day happenings of Shrewsbury and Brother Cadfael's home abbey of Saint Peter and Paul, and which I for one absolutely and hugely appreciate and majorly enjoy, and furthermore, how the English history parts Ellis Peters provides in and with her Brother Cadfael mysteries, these are superbly authentic, these are realistic and as such solid historical fiction and not annoyingly fantastical. 

And while some readers seem to consider Peters' heavy-duty history lesson like beginning for her 1984 Dead Man's Ransom (for the ninth of the Brother Cadfael novels) something negative and something to criticise (basically being too much information-dropping so to speak), I for one in fact hugely appreciate this, with me actually finding the beginning of Dead Man's Ransom and its solidly detailed historicity both supremely interesting as well as massively educational and as such also the part of Dead Man's Ransom I have enjoyed the most, since the murder of Sheriff Gilbert Prescott in Dead Man's Ransom, discovering whodunit, the Romeo and Juliet like romance of Elis and Milicent Prescote and in particular how Ellis Peters has Dead Man's Ransom conclude is just not all that much to my personal reading tastes (and by any stretch of my imagination). 

For yes, Dead Man's Ransom (after the personally speaking absolutely delightfully enlightening historical introduction) is simply too convoluted for me, with Peters' story featuring too many characters and also too many possible murder suspects (at least three of them). And while I guess I kind of am happy that I could not easily guess how Eluid was the villain, I also and completely do most definitely consider the ending of Dead Man's Ransom rather majorly problematic. For because in Dead Man's Ransom Ellis Peters has Eliud smother Sheriff Gilbert Prestcote to death not out of battlefield necessity, self-defence or because Prescote is somehow evilly nasty, but cold-bloodedly and calculatedly to clear romantic obstacles for his foster brother Elis and equally so for himself, I honestly and indeed find it supremely grating as well as quite emotionally upsetting that both Brother Cadfael and Hugh Beringar in Dead Man's Ransom allow Eluid to completely escape a prison sentence for his deed. 

Sure, Ellis Peters prioritising natural justice (aligning true love and restoring social harmony) in her Brother Cadfael novels over the harsh, punitive laws of the 12th century, which would have meant a brutal public execution for Eliud in Dead Man's Ransom is something with which I wholeheartedly agree, sorry, but no legal consequences at all for Eluid just rubs me the wrong proverbial way. So yes and indeed, while I have still more than decently enjoyed Dead Man's Ransom, Peters' ending for Dead Man's Ransom of allowing Eliud to completely escape even somewhat, even a bit of prison sentence (or some financial penalties) leaves a somewhat bitter taste for me, since I do strongly believe that mercy should temper justice and never ever completely erase it (so that my rating for Dead Man's Ransom is therefore only three stars and that said three star rating is also pretty generous of and from me).
