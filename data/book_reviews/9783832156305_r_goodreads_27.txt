Rating: 3/5

This is the third Houellebecq I’ve read, which I suspect is exactly when his tricks begin to show. The first time, it feels sharp, almost prophetic. The second, deliberate. By the third, you start to notice the pattern — the same quiet despair, the same clinical detachment, the same drift into sex, loneliness, tourism, markets, bodies… yada yada, yaca yaca, you get the idea.

That said, it’s not a bad book. It moves. It holds your attention in that strangely numb way his novels always do, as if you’re being guided through a life that already knows how it ends. His protagonists — always men, always slightly emptied out — seem less like individuals and more like variations of the same condition: a kind of resigned consciousness. Not quite wanting to die, but not especially committed to living either. Just… continuing. Observing. Consuming.

There are moments here that feel almost indecent in how blunt they are — scenes that lean into the erotic or the transactional with a kind of cold curiosity. Sometimes it works, sometimes it feels like repetition. And maybe that’s where it lost me a little: the sense of inevitability became too clear, too early. The moment Valérie entered the story and the relationship began to take shape, I felt I already knew where it was heading. Not in a clever, tragic inevitability kind of way — just in a familiar, almost mechanical one. It drained some of the tension, made the emotional arc feel less discovered and more… confirmed.

But what’s interesting is how all his books start to blur into one long, extended argument about modern life — predictable, flattened, quietly tragic. Even when something like love appears, it doesn’t feel like salvation. More like a brief flicker. A small, improbable warmth in an otherwise controlled temperature.

And yet — I can see how this might feel like a five-star read if it were your first encounter with him. There’s something undeniably compelling in the way he arranges disillusionment. But having been here before, it lands differently. Still engaging, still sharp in places, but no longer surprising. It doesn’t expand his world; it confirms it.

I admired it more than I loved it. Which, with Houellebecq, increasingly feels like the most honest response.
