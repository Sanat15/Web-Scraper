Rating: 4/5

This book is probably best aimed at the 9-12 age reader, and yet -- like all well-written books -- it will appeal to many older readers. It is Judith Kerr's memoir of being a German/Jewish refugee as a child, and it is both fascinating and poignant. Although the author sticks to facts and memories, as much as possible, the book is presented as fiction -- she distances herself with the third-person point-of-view, and by referring to herself as "Anna" (which actually was one of her names, as she explains in the Author's Note).

As a popular and well-known critic of Hitler's Nazi party, Judith's father Alfred Kerr was at the top of Hitler's enemy list. Tipped off by a sympathetic policeman, Kerr left Berlin for Prague the week before the election which swept the Nazi party into totalitarian power. Judith, her older brother and mother left the day before the election; just in the knick of time, it turns out, as their passports would have been confiscated the next day. The family then become refugees, moving from Switzerland to Paris and then finally to England -- where they settle. Anna, from a child's point of view, describes each new world -- and her experiences of learning how to fit in, make friends, negotiate a different educational system, and in the case of France, learn a new language. For the most part, Anna presents a positive, optimistic view of the change in her circumstances . . . for her, it is all an adventure. An adult reading the book will interpret things rather differently, particularly as the family's economic situation becomes ever more precarious. There are a few moments of true sadness -- the fate of their friend Onkel Julius comes to mind -- but you rarely feel an adult consciousness impinging on the perspective of the more innocent Anna. There are hints of the worsening situation for German Jews in the years of 1933-36, but the book doesn't really touch on the horrors to come.
