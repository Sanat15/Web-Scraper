Rating: 4/5

Sausage Hunter

Too Many Cooks is a novel whose broth is anything but spoiled, and this is not so much due to its cooking time as to its ingredients. Take
-	A Nero Wolfe who is out of his element this time because he has to leave his brownhouse building in order to follow an invitation as a guest of honour to a meeting of Les Quinze Maîtres, a group of renowned chefs who meet all five years to compete, bicker and puzzle each other with their best creations. Not only does Wolfe have to undergo the tortures of a train journey but at his destiny, he also has to put up with furniture too small for him, and then he is shot at, into the bargain, while practising a dinner speech in his yellow pyjamas. Small wonder that everything he yearns for is to go home as soon as possible so that he eventually announces, ”I’m going to expose the murderer, and demonstrate his guilt, before we leave this room”, leaving the reader wondering whether Wolfe will eat his words, which would prove quite a disappointment after all the delicacies he has eaten before.
-	An especially witty Archie Goodwin functioning as the narrative voice, offering amusing remarks about Wolfe’s quirks, the beauty of the countryside, which is not too impressive for him as a town-rooted man, and all the ado made over food by the various chefs.
-	An unpopular murder victim, whose body is found with a knife in its back in the course of one of the competitions concocted by the chefs. This man, Paul Laszio, has stolen one man’s wife, another man’s job and a third man’s recipe, and all the men in question are present on the scene of the murder.
-	Wolfe’s usual tendency to sometimes cynical, sometimes wise observations, such as, ”You know very well what life consists of, it consists of the humanities, and among them there is a decent and intelligent control of the appetites which we share with dogs. A man doesn’t wolf a carcass or howl on a hillside from dark to dawn; he eats well-cooked food, when he can get it, in judicious quantities; and he suits his ardor to his wise convenience.” I liked this one especially because it happens to agree with my view that the merit of being a decent human being does not lie in never feeling low impulses but in bridling them. Wolfe also shares some thoughts about the achievements of American cooking with regard to la haute cuisine, which are interesting in that they are relatively short and concise and well-woven into the plot. I must confess that these observations and cynical remarks on Wolfe’s part are the chief reason I have fallen in love with the series. Here is another one, ”If anyone not a moron has determined to kill your husband, he will be killed. Nothing is simpler than to kill a man; the difficulties arise in attempting to avoid the consequences.”
-	A locked-room murder mystery which is not as easy to solve as it seems at first.
-	Some serious observations on race relations and equality: Whereas the local public prosecutor and the sheriff denigrate the black staff by using the then rife racial slurs, Wolfe treats them with decency and respect regardless of the hostility this earns him from Sheriff Pettigrew, and he even casually rebukes Archie when his assistant fails to do so. In this context, Wolfe makes another of his clever observations, one that should be well-remembered in our age of identity politics and wokeism: “The ideal human agreement is one in which distinctions of race and color and religion are totally disregarded; anyone helping to preserve those distinctions is postponing that ideal.”
-	Saucisse minuit, a delicacy devised by one of the chefs, the recipe for which Wolfe would so much like to obtain for his private use only that he undertook to leave his familiar surroundings and dare to brave the vicissitudes of travel in the first place. 

With these ingredients, thrown in in the right doses, Too Many Cooks will doubtless go down well as a culinary murder mystery.
