Rating: 3/5

This opens with an account of the actual shoot-out at the OK Corral (or near the OK Corral as it actually happened) in almost mind-numbing and gory detail, and I almost didn't keep reading but I found the book got better the further I got. The best was saved for last in a final accounting of the two rival groups -- Old Man Clanton's and the Earp -- and then a fascinating epilogue to all of the main character's lives as they outlived their glory days and settled, more or less kicking, into a modernizing world. 

This is indeed better than most accounts, and actually attempts to draw in more of the women's stories with some sympathy. The casual racism, however, was hard to take. Directly vocalized by racist characters or descriptive of their thinking, sure, has to be done. Those crazy white folks were most of them crazy racists, and that needs to be remembered. But I found it uncomfortably rife throughout descriptions as well, starting with the support for the Confederacy and continuing on through descriptions of breeds, half-breeds, Mexicans and greasers. There's an argument I know, for saturating everything with a world view, but it is too normalized here, to uncomfortably like a position we should be comfortable with. I spose for all I know it is a position the author and the readers are comfortable with, but I'll give them the benefit of the doubt. 

My only last criticism as someone who knows Tombstone and a few of its old inhabitants well, is that it's a lot smaller, tawdrier, sadder to me. But that's personal I guess. The Birdcage has no faded grandeur, it's a heartbreaking place. And folks I knew from there, miners still, were decent and had wicked senses of humour, seems to me that has always been part of the western story too. Or maybe just among the Mexicans and Indians, the conquering greedy hordes had no time for such things.
