Rating: 2/5

Disappointing. The book starts with a sanctimonious complaint about Lauren: he ended up not participating, according to Gross, because he feared the book would linger on his affair and be more interested in gossip than his actual story. Tssk-tssk. Of course, this complaint is undercut a bit by the book's own cover, which managed to use the word "dishy" five times - kinda proving Lauren's point.

Who is Ralph Lauren? The book doesn't really seem to know. It tells us the story of Ralph Lauren, polytropos: is he basically a copyist, inarticulate and dithering? Or a ruthless businessman of singular vision, who can spend hours discussing the details of buttons and plackets, pushing through his will even if nobody else considers it a good idea? A loner, uncomfortable around customers - or the leader of a gang of friends, inspiring loyalty since childhood, a guy who spent his off hours telling potential clients how to fold their ties just so? A guy who promotes favorites based only on looks and style - or a CEO who always seems to have ended up with precisely the right people to move his business forward?

All these Laurens seem incompatible, yet Gross neatly arranges this wall of portraits chronologically in the hope that something coherent emerges. It doesn't. You get the impression that Gross just wrote down whatever his different sources told him, without too much concern for their various biases or trying to reconcile different impressions.

Who does Gross think Lauren is? What is this book trying to say about him? What exactly does it reveal? I have no idea, and I don't know if Gross knows either.
