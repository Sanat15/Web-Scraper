Rating: 5/5

Chester Himes wrote this in 1937 while doing his own bit at Ohio State Penitentiary, then watched a publisher bowdlerize it into Cast the First Stone in 1952, cutting the queer heart clean out of the chest. Sixty-one years and one dead author later, the uncut version walked back through the gates as Yesterday Will Make You Cry. The book about a man kept from what he wanted spent decades getting kept from what it wanted to say. The sentence, fittingly, ran twenty to twenty-five.

Jimmy Monroe, convict 57232, comes in at nineteen with a robbery jacket, a college dropout's vocabulary, and a private theology built from the Iliad, dividing the world into runners and the men chasing them. He has decided that pain he skips thinking about can leave him untouched. Every wall Jimmy builds around his feelings gets waterlogged from within. The man so certain he could out-stubborn twenty years learns that stone outlasts stubbornness every time.

A convict from F-5, Badeye, bleeds out on a gray sheet in bubbles graded fine to coarse. Another convict rams his own head down a commode ahead of the flames. The great fire of 1930 reads as a page torn from Dante, three hundred-odd men wrapped in the very blankets they begged for all winter and could hardly use, while Jimmy runs across the corpses stepping in guts, tallying his failure to be Achilles.

Himes files these horrors flat and cold, which makes them colder. He knows the gap between a writer telling you a thing was terrible and a witness counting the buckets.

What lifts the book past a catalogue of atrocity is Rico. The gypsy kid with the busted kneecaps, the ukelele, the mother in Los Angeles, and the sneer. He wobbles in and walks off with the whole novel. Their love is the realest thing in a place engineered to make everything fake, and Himes handles it with a tenderness that would embarrass a lesser man out of the attempt. Jimmy keeps insisting it rises above the slime around them. Rico keeps painting him into the stars and begging to hear that he is right. The space between those two hopes is where the heartbreak lives.

The dormitory votes with poison notes. Old captain Charlie, once posted to feed mice on a lonely wall, gets squeezed into charging them both, and Jimmy picks the charge over the pardon on his way out the door. Being a man once beats waiting to be one later.

Jimmy burns five years proving he feels a blank and one autumn discovering he feels everything. He came hunting a hero to be and found instead that he could love somebody and let him down in the same breath. That he could take a fall for a friend and still leave him waving both arms through the bars.

Himes, who served seven years for armed robbery before becoming an acclaimed novelist, created a work expanding beyond prison literature into an existential question about freedom's nature. 

The text's psychological depth makes Camus' The Stranger seem shallow, while its institutional critique equals Solzhenitsyn's Gulag Archipelago in exposing how confinement reveals society's contradictions. 

Monroe's observation that "every man in here built his own prison long before the state built this one" connects across cultures, from Dostoyevsky's House of the Dead to Genet's Miracle of the Rose. 

This work represents Himes' personal triumph, a literary achievement that, like its central character, overcomes circumstances to approach wisdom. As Monroe concludes in a moment of clarity: "Yesterday will make you cry, but tomorrow depends on what you do with those tears today."

Memory is the sentence inside the sentence, the term past all parole. Jimmy does his time on top of each moment. The past drives you crazy and the future kills you, yet the past crawls back anyway, wearing his mother's cracked hands and Rico's crushed-strawberry mouth. Read it for the love story America locked in solitary across half a century. Read it because Himes served the time and paid for every word.
