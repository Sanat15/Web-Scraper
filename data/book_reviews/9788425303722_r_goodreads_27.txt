Rating: 4/5

As a Ukrainian living through an active war, this was not an easy book to read. While reading The Dark Arena, I often imagined what my country might look like if it were fully occupied or after the war finally ends. The ruined cities, displaced people and emotional devastation feel very real.

One moment that struck me was when the priest suggested that Germany’s ruins would remain forever. Reading this in 2026, knowing that Germany became one of Europe’s most prosperous countries, was a reminder of how difficult it is to imagine recovery in the middle of destruction. But it also gave me hope. 

I was also fascinated by Puzo’s portrayal of Germans who continued to support Nazi ideas after the war. The professor’s insistence that his son remained human despite his crimes showed how difficult it is to abandon beliefs that are deeply tied to identity. It made me think about russia. Even if the current regime collapses, many people may continue to cling to ideas of national superiority. And that is also why it’s important to understand it is not only putin’s war as many of people may state.

The ending highlighted one of war’s greatest tragedies: soldiers who survive physically but cannot return to the lives they once had. The war continues long after the fighting ends, both for veterans and for the families who care for them. Ukraine is already building institutions to support rehabilitation and mental health, but some wounds cannot simply be healed. Many people will spend the rest of their lives trying to return to a version of normality that no longer exists. I can only hope for the happy ending for our soldiers, not the one that Puzo described.
