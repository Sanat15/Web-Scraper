Rating: 3/5

There’s a roughness to Privateers that makes it feel very different from some of Bova’s later Grand Tour novels. It’s much more openly adventure-driven—corporate conflict, sabotage, piracy, lunar politics, economic warfare—and for a while that energy works in the book’s favor. The story moves quickly, the stakes are clear, and the setting still carries that optimistic-but-precarious feeling early hard science fiction about the near-future Solar System often had.

The Moon itself remains one of the stronger aspects of the novel. Bova has always been good at making space settlements feel industrial and practical rather than sleek or romanticized. Lunar society here feels tense, competitive, heavily commercialized, and dependent on fragile systems that could collapse under political or economic pressure at any moment. There’s a believable frontier atmosphere running through the whole thing.

The conflict between corporations and independent operators also gives the story most of its momentum. The idea that economic warfare in space would eventually blur into outright piracy feels fairly plausible, especially in a future where governments have weakened and resource control matters more than ideology. Some of the logistics around transport, mining, and orbital infrastructure were genuinely enjoyable too.

At the same time, this is definitely one of the more uneven entries in the Grand Tour universe. The characters often feel thinner than the setting surrounding them, and several interactions come across more like plot mechanics than believable emotional exchanges. The dialogue especially can become stiff or overly functional in places.

There’s also a kind of pulpy simplicity to the conflict that occasionally works against the novel. Certain antagonists feel underdeveloped, motivations can become predictable, and a lot of the tension resolves in ways that feel a little too convenient. After a while, it becomes fairly easy to guess where most of the major confrontations are heading.

And like quite a few older Bova novels, some of the gender dynamics and interpersonal writing haven’t aged particularly well. Certain attitudes and character interactions feel very rooted in the era the book was written, sometimes enough to briefly pull attention away from the stronger scientific and political material.

Still, the book remains entertaining largely because Bova understands how to make near-future space exploration feel tangible. Even when the characterization falters, there’s usually another interesting detail about lunar industry, corporate expansion, or life in hostile environments waiting around the corner.

What carried the novel for me was less the individual plot twists and more the broader sense of humanity beginning to spread outward in messy, imperfect ways. Space here isn’t utopian. It’s commercialized, dangerous, politically unstable, and full of competing interests—which honestly makes the setting feel more believable than a cleaner version probably would have.
