Rating: 4/5

the last opium den by nick tosches is a hidden gem in the caves of the obscure and taboo world of “opium literature.” the work itself was not only unique and fresh in its own right but also beautifully commending and reminiscent of all the opium literature that came before it. i’m so pleased to have not only found, but have been able to read a 21st century account of a traditional opium experience that i, along with the rest of the modern world, thought had been lost in time. 

 i loved all of tosches’s nods to many previous works of this genre, such as the broader tongue-in-cheek sensationalism of asia (popular in 1800s-1900s works of both fiction and non-fiction) as well as the many similarities (whether intentional or unintentional) to other works that came before it, namely thomas de quincey’s “the confessions of an english opium eater-” my favorite being the brief and language barried but deeply intimate relationship between the author and a fellow opium consumer (tosches’s thai and de quincey’s malay). tosches’s critiques of the faux bourgeois indulgences of today were quite random at first but tied so perfectly into his overall message and the philosophy detailed as the piece progressed. 

 though, at the end of the work, tosches found himself in a dilapidated shack- the complete opposite of the romantic velvet draped opium den he had sought out and called into question the validity of his opulent fantasy he harbored in the first place. he seemed to have settled on the idea that what he had imagined in his head had been unrealistic and nonexistent- however, i wish he could have been informed before his death that such ornate and elegant places did in fact exist during the late 1800s, albeit, for the wealthy- namely, on the flower boats of the pearl river and secluded flats in both france and london. though, its remarkable that he was able to navigate southeast asia and find himself in a genuine opium den in the end. 

also, if anyone has a pdf of “the big smoke: the chinese art &amp; craft of opium“ by peter lee please please send it over.
