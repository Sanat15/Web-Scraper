Rating: 2/5

"White critics loved Home to Harlem," writes Belinda Edmondson in her introduction. It was also the first bestseller by a Black author, fueled in large part by white readers. And unfortunately, what I kept thinking while reading is: yeah this does seem like the type of book white readers love because it confirms their biases about a minority group. Just like bestselling books about Jews tend to be about them dying in the Holocaust, or bestselling books about life in India center terrible poverty: yeah, white readers love to have confirmation from a Black author that Harlem is full of the oversexed and undereducated. Meanwhile, the character who feels most like an author insert -- educated, a Caribbean immigrant -- has this said about him: "Life burned in Ray perhaps more intensely than in Jake [the Black American protagonist]. Ray felt more and his range was wider and he could not be satisfied with the easy, simple things that sufficed for Jake." He's not like other girls Black men, guys! 

McKay writes some beautiful and vivid descriptions of Harlem in the spring, but I found this scattershot novel to be, overall, unpleasant and off-putting -- and I haven't even touched on the way McKay writes women; let's just say his descriptions ratchet all of his disdain up to 11, with a lot of value put on fuckability. And I just can't shake my impression of this as a self-hating book.

Basically: W. E. B. Du Bois hated this novel, and as Annie Lennox said: who am I to disagree?
