Rating: 4/5

The founding novel of literature is the one that will inspire all future narratives. We are familiar with the story of Ulysses; we have read a few episodes and seen a film, but reading the entire work, with its lengthy chapters, Homeric epithets, and detailed descriptions of the marine world, including its monsters and demons, is a different experience. One has the impression of attending a closed session on the sea. Yet, Ulysses only longs to better take to the sea as if the mainland forbade him, and as if his condemnation to wandering on the sea was more beneficial on the water than on land. And then there are the episodes worthy of an adventure novel, such as the Sirens, symbols of literary song, the author's voice that takes us where we want to go and can destroy us (hello, Bovary), and monsters like Charybdis and Scylla.
Finally, this is surprising; we talk more about the others — Telemachus, her son looking for him, and Penelope waiting for him — than about him, who wants to return. The novel has almost everything: the search for the other, the quest, the song of words, and the pleasure of returning home. It is as enjoyable to study and understand, so full of symbols, as reading.
