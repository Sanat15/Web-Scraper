Rating: 4/5

I picked up this book years ago in a library sale - it's the original cloth-covered book published in 1956. I have a box of those old books and challenged myself to read them all instead of giving them away because they're "old." I'm glad to have read this one.

It took a while for me to get into this one - I was convinced it was old-fashioned and not much of a story. The more I got into the character studies of Dr. Abelman and brash New York marketer, Woody Thrasher, the more engrossing the story was. I didn't particularly like Thrasher at first, but he evolved during the story, mainly because of the harsh, loud, bigoted, but big-hearted Dr. Abelman.

I'm amazed, but not surprised for the time, of the perception of the black community and off-handed treatment of women. You can almost see an inkling of the evolution of acceptance and understanding coming, though (albeit it not for years afterwards). The story evolved into a fascinating development of all of the characters' traits and personalities and you find yourself accepting them for who they are.

A truly great story of a gruff Jewish doctor in a rough part of the city, who wouldn't have lived any other way than to help anyone who needed it for a two-dollar fee, even as his medical cronies go on to be well-off and live comfortably. The story of Thrasher, a man who openly cheats on his wife, skeptical of the powers that be and sure of his 'genius' in the industry, slowly changing throughout the story and realizing life can mean so many things to so many people. Myron, Dr. Abelman's goofy nephew, a young man with aspirations who probably will never catch a break, but will keep trying. Herman, a young black gang leader, who is cared for by the doctor, whether he wanted it or not. Just a few of the characters that blend to create a satisfying, meaningful end. 

Sometimes tough reading, I'm glad I picked this one up. It will be a book remembered and thought about for a long time.
