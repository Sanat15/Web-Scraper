Rating: 3/5

This is a good book, and it has real insights, but I would not call it a classic history of the Korean War. The original subtitle, a lesson in unpreparedness, feels more accurate than how it is sometimes branded now. A major theme throughout is that the U.S. military was not prepared for the kind of ground fight it ended up in, and that unreadiness shows up directly in casualty levels. I did find the discussion of the MacArthur–Truman conflict useful, and the diplomatic material at the end of the book was genuinely strong and clarifying.

That said, the book is very much a product of its time, and the tone often feels dated. It was written only about 10 to 15 years after the war, and there is a lot of editorializing that is asserted rather than supported. The lack of references compared to modern nonfiction makes the author’s own voice stand out even more. This shows up in questionable factual claims, like the suggestion that South Korea could reach 120 degrees, and in ideological tells, including the use of the term “War Between the States” for the Civil War, which appears three separate times... this was "unconventional" to say the least.

Where the book is most distinctive is in its descriptions of combat. It notably names individual soldiers who were killed and goes into detail about what they were doing, what the mission was, and how they died. A lot of the ground fighting described involves repeated hill assaults, with sustained attention on the sequence of advance, fire, and death. The technological picture on the ground feels more equal than people often assume, with broadly comparable infantry weapons, while U.S. advantages lie mainly in air, naval power, and communications. At the same time, the limits of 1950s airpower matter: reduced night visibility and limited precision help explain why fronts and battle lines remained central and why numerical superiority still mattered so much. The book also does not shy away from describing brutality, including executions and deception tactics like uniform changes and fighting in civilian clothing.

By the end, I thought the book finished strong. The diplomatic sections and broader reflections on civil–military relations were insightful, and the analogies to legions and centurions landed better than earlier editorial passages. Overall, there are real truths and useful perspectives here, especially on preparedness, ground combat, and the limits of airpower in this era. I just do not think this is the definitive history of the Korean War, and the heavy tactical detail combined with dated tone limits it. For me, that puts it at a solid three out of five.
