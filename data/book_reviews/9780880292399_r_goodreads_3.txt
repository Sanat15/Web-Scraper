Rating: 5/5

I’m actually surprised there aren’t more reviews of this. Let me say this unequivocally: you cannot consider yourself a serious historian of any kind without owning this. I am 44 years old. I bought this as a 20-year-old soldier while stationed at Fort Hood, Texas. I have taken it with me everywhere I’ve gone since then, and it’s still in great shape—amazingly.

Let me get down to it. This thing gives you a perspective on the scale of time across historical eras like nothing else can. You unfold it on your floor (if your living room or hallway is long enough), and it stretches to 4.5 meters (nearly 15 feet) in length. You literally “walk through time” as you pace back and forth between the Middle Ages, the modern era, and the ancient world. You see, while staring down the long expanse of paper, that the pyramids were more “ancient” to Julius Caesar than the Roman Colosseum is to us today. As I said, the scale and relative placement of events it provides are essential for any historian who needs this perspective as a foundation for understanding the past. Needless to say, when I bought it back in 2001, I was instantly enamored of it—and still am. Every time I unfold its 15 feet of unbelievably dense information, I feel an immediate sense of awe. I’ll probably never examine every part in detail, and that brings me to my next point.

The timeline is incredibly detailed. It traces the development of every major civilization or political entity from the dawn of civilization (4004 BC), branching like a family tree. The branches expand with territorial conquest and split where territories are divided or broken up by dynasties, such as after the deaths of Clovis and Alexander. You can see how Rome grew from a small republic city-state into a Western world–dominating empire that leaves a huge imprint across the chart, only to begin visibly breaking apart again, with large regions like Britain falling away almost like a cell dividing after mitosis. It’s remarkable. It’s hard to even write about this without getting carried away, but there is one last point worth mentioning.

Yes, this wall chart is dated in certain respects, but it is still largely accurate. Off the cuff, I’d say it is about 99% accurate in the placement of dates and the naming of rulers. It was originally created by a Christian scholar in the Victorian era. The pyramids are not placed correctly—the Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2600 BC over a period of about 26 years, but the chart places it closer to 1800 BC. That’s not a major issue. Knowing this, you can still appreciate it. In fact, most of the inaccuracies occur in the very early periods before 1000 BC. Biblical figures and patriarchs leading up to Jesus are also included, with figures like Moses spanning hundreds of years, as if the Bible were taken literally. That’s acceptable in context: while not historically accurate, it reflects the biblical narrative itself, and for those who study the Bible as literature, it can be a useful reference. So I don’t necessarily consider the inclusion of biblical figures to be a flaw in a timeline of world history. Finally, yes, it is clearly Western-centric. Chinese civilization is included along the bottom, but there is limited coverage beyond that. It is, for the most part, a timeline of Western civilization.
