“Some day you will find out that there is far more happiness in another's happiness than in your own.”

“Father Goriot,” a cornerstone of Balzac’s “The Human Comedy,” is a merciless dissection of the Parisian society’s moral bankruptcy. Balzac's characters, move through the narrative with a sense of inevitability, their destinies intertwined in a web of deception and betrayal. Father Goriot, a broken patriarch consumed by the flames of paternal love, languishes in the squalor of his own making, a tragic figure cast adrift in a sea of moral decay. 

Paris, a sprawling beast of stone and shadow, a maze of light and dark, where dreams are bought and sold, where every soul has a price. The young, they come to this place, seeking fortune, seeking fame. The old, they cling to memories to ghosts of what was or what could have been. The boarding house, a microcosm of this grand tapestry, its rooms filled with the whispers of the lost and the longing, where each soul is a thread, frayed and worn, yet holding fast to the fabric of this city. Here, the old man Goriot lays his head, among the driftwood of humanity. 

Goriot, once a man of substance, now reduced to the echo of footsteps in empty halls. The city moves around him, a river of faces, none he knows. His heart, a dry well, echoes with the memory of love. Rastignac, the youth, hungry for the world, his eyes wide to the horizon. Rastignac, the seeker, he walks these dreams, his eyes open, his heart torn between the light of virtue and the darkness of desire. He sees the old man, sees what it costs to love in a land that loves only gold. A lesson written in the lines of Goriot's face. Vautrin, the watcher, the schemer. He knows the currency of souls, trades in the dark. His laughter, a thing of shadows, it follows Rastignac. Tells him truths the boy don't want to hear, about the world, about himself. Anastasie and Delphine, daughters of Goriot, once cradled in his arms, now but ghosts in his fading mind. They walk through the grand salons of Paris, each step a betrayal, each smile a lie, silk and satin trailing behind them, laughter chimes in the gilded air. They come to him, their father, when the sun dips low, and their hands are empty. He gives, and they take. Silver and gold, the price of his heart, his flesh and blood. The old man's love, a river run dry, and they, they are the desert, ever thirsty, never filled. 

Paris, a world unto itself, a universe of fevered dreams of splendor and squalor, where nobility is but a mask, and poverty the true face beneath. This Paris, it is a crucible, where the fates of Goriot and his daughters, of Rastignac and Vautrin, are forged. A place of transformation, where the old world dies and a new one struggles to be born, where every life is a story, and every story is but a piece of the whole, a note in the requiem and the hymn of "Father Goriot." The old man's end, it comes quiet, a candle snuffed in the dawn. Rastignac’s changed. Can't say if it's for better or worse. The city doesn’t care. It moves on…
