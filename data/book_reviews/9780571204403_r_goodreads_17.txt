Rating: 4/5

Sometimes going into a book with low expectations sets you up for success--that was surely the case in this book, considered by many to be Ishiguro's weakest. And personally, I just don't see it! I think he's as skillful here as he is in any one of his other novels and presents another potentially unreliable narrator who is deeply impacted by childhood trauma and doing what he can to restore order to his life.

Christopher Banks is a young English boy living in Shanghai with his parents in the early 1900s when they both go missing. Considered potential foul play due to his mother's vocal outrage against the import of opium to the country, the kidnappings go unsolved and Banks grows up wanting to be a detective and solve the case himself. He does indeed become a detective, but back in England. And eventually begins to try and uncover the mystery of his parents' disappearance which along the way excavates more hidden truths from his past.

Of course, Ishiguro's prose is flawless. I think he could write nearly anything and I'd appreciate it at a sentence-level, at the very least. But the pacing and story structure here are interesting and kept me engaged throughout despite this not being a particularly action-packed mystery, as one might expect from the conventions of the genre. It's a detective story, but the real mystery for the reader is understanding the truth of Banks' life as he sees it versus as it really was. 

Themes of the faulty memories and self-deception, of the coping mechanisms formed after childhood trauma, the disasters of colonialism, and the failure to live up to expectations imposed by one's self or perceived expectations from others are all neatly woven into this novel in a way that had me wondering what it was *truly* about until the last chapter. I thought the conclusion was powerful and heartbreaking, as most of his stories' finales are. Another excellent addition to Ishiguro's oeuvre.
