Rating: 4/5

‘There’s no horse like him in the whole world. He’s a bright red bay with a black mane and tail. He has a white cross on his forehead and four white socks that are all even to the last inch. He stands over sixteen hands and he’s perfect from head to tail.’ 

A small book with a big heart.
Actually, a pretty big book with an even bigger heart.
It is more than a book for children. It is a celebration of this wonderful, hard working and steadfast companion to man and a memorial to the unsung heroes of the Great War, horses and people on both sides of the no-man’s land whose sacrifice should have been a lesson to future generations about the absurdity of war and the wholesale slaughter it brings.

 Lifeline of the whole army, you said, sir. 

&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;

So, what’s it all about?

 A yearling colt and a young lad have more in common than awkward gawkishness. 

A yearling is sold at auction in Devon, traumatically separated from his mother. The farmer is a bit of a drunkard and in debt, but he has a young son named Albert who falls in love with the young horse the moment he lays eyes on him. He calls him Joey.
Albert and Joey work together on the farm, until the start of the war. Then the father sells Joey to the army in order to cover some pressing debt. Joey crosses the Channel with a cavalry regiment and experiences both field charges and devastating attacks against barbed wire and machine guns. Later, Joey is taken prisoner and put to hard work in field hospitals and at heavy gun transport.
The rest of the story you can discover reading the book.

&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;

It would take a real Grinch to be mean and find fault in the way the story is told. The book is written at a school level of comprehension, but it is not condescending or cute. The theme doesn’t allow for such treatments. I think it is quite difficult for a writer to get the tone and the vocabulary right for a younger audience: children are more intelligent, more perceptive and sophisticated than we often give them credit for. Seeing the war through the eye of an innocent animal is a good way to learn about this past trauma.
I haven’t read anything by Morpurgo before this novel, nor have I seen the Spielberg movie adaptation, but I never felt any of the awkwardness (cringe reaction) that I got from some recent YA and children stories. This one felt very natural, unforced and even restrained, given the often brutal war scenes that need to be told.

 And then you’ll see a horse that understands every word that’s said to him. 

I did stumble a bit on the proposition that a yearling colt can perfectly understand spoken English and, later in the story, foreign speech in German and French. But I also sort of understand why Morpurgo did it: Joey the horse sees no difference between the English soldiers, the German ones or the farmers of Flanders. They are all human, caught in the deadly grip of the war machine. Joey doesn’t care about the origin of the conflict, about politics or maps. He wants to help those in need and he wants to please his friends, those who treat him with kindness and respect.
And he wants to be reunited with his best human, Albert.

&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;&gt;&gt;&gt;&lt;&lt;&lt;

I liked the book, despite not being its target audience. I grew up in a big city and had no contact with horses in my childhood. I kind of miss the kind of connection that I read about in books. I think that I want next to read a novel by another author who is renowned for his passion for the noble creatures: Dick Francis.
