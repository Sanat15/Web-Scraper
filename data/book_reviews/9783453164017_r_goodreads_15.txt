Rating: 4/5

Possession: A Romance is indeed a challenging read, filled with numerous names and references, along with lengthy poems in a Victorian style that is solemn and grand but difficult to digest.

It wasn't until I reached page 50 that I felt truly engaged.

The story itself is quite conventional and passionate, unveiling a hidden love through a letter. The remarkable aspect remains Byatt’s literary prowess and deep literary knowledge, which are profoundly impressive.

I Googled a lot about this book, eager to know who the poet in the story was, and whether such a person existed in reality. But for the first time, I found that information on a book was so scarce on the all-knowing internet!

There were only a few links, and I eventually found that it won the 1990 Booker Prize. It was adapted into a movie in 2002. I couldn't find the identity of Randolph Ash, nor any relevant reviews. Only in a forum did I discover that many people praised the book while agreeing it was very difficult to read.

As the book progresses, it becomes more engaging, driven by the urge to uncover its mysteries, making it hard to put down. The shocking aspect of this love story is the deep sense of loneliness of Christabel, who insists on celibacy, refusing to be violated, encasing herself in layers of protective armor, and striving to preserve her lifelong reputation. She is proud, unwilling to follow common customs or bow down. Despite falling in love, she refuses to give herself up. When she becomes pregnant, she leaves without a trace, preventing our poet from finding her. Stubbornly, she doesn't even reveal her pregnancy to her cousin, with whom she lives. Despite everyone seeing her growing belly, she remains unchanged. On the day of childbirth, she chooses to face it alone, and her cousin doesn't know whether the child survives. It wasn't until the last few pages that I learned the child survived and lived with her as her niece. This girl never liked the lonely aunt, not knowing that the strange "old maid" was her mother, and even hated poetry, despite her parents being outstanding poets.

What a strange story. During their lifetimes, no one besides Christabel's sister knew the child's true identity. Our poor poet Ash died with confusion and regret. His wife was also an odd person.

Yes, the story is quite strange. Not until the last few pages did I understand it fully and overturn all previous assumptions. Was Ash's betrayal truly a betrayal? His wife had always refused conjugal relations, yet Ash still respected and cared for her deeply. He had no scandal except for this hidden, tragic love. In terms of a true marital relationship, wasn't Christabel more of Ash's wife?

Christabel's departure was fueled by her deadly loneliness and external factors like Ash's wife and her cohabiting girlfriend's suicide. Before the discovery of these letters, all scholars assumed she was a lesbian, and her poetry was based on feminism. Ash, meanwhile, was a rational, reserved, self-talking, and obscure poet. But these letters overturned everything, and the study of their poetry would restart.

Anyone's creations are profoundly influenced by personal experiences, and love is fatal in this respect.

Some past people, and some in the present, study the past, and the overturning of the past destroys the present. Reconstruct, then destroy again.

Exploring someone's life is challenging. Some secrets, if one wishes to hide, can be hidden for a lifetime. Autobiographies selectively hide secrets, and biographies are even less reliable. How can someone else explore another person's inner world? Wilde said that if the universe is finite, the human heart is infinite. An infinite heart, described and recorded with finite words, is an attempt to sketch and metaphorically represent.

Christabel used metaphors to hide a deeply entangled love in beautiful, unique poetry. Ellen Ash (Ash's wife) used deletion to hide pain in dull diaries. Only letters taken to the grave and passionate love letters tell the true story in the underworld.

3.9 / 5 stars
