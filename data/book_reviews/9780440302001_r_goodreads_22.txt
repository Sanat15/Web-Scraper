To love someone is to know them, but more importantly to care to know them. And thus, in turn, to love oneself is to truly know oneself. James Baldwin explores these ideas through the messy entangled lives of a group of artists in 1960s NYC in this incredible novel of passion, identity, and longing.

Somehow, as only a master novelist like Baldwin can, he manages to depict the raw and realistic lives of so many clashing individuals in profound clarity. Spanning romantic and platonic relationships that diverge in race, class, gender, and sexuality, Another Country is both a time capsule and a timeless piece of literature about one of the form’s most universal topics: love.

At times I seriously hated these characters because Baldwin did such a good job at crafting them as these horrid and hypocritical people. But because he treats nearly all his characters this way, using them as a sort of mirror to society, the reader, and even himself at times, he shows just how judgmental and unforthcoming we can be as well. It hurts, but it’s effective.

It’s remarkable to me that this book even got published in its day, not because it didn’t deserve to be but because it’s so brutal and honest about topics that, if they are taboo in certain places in the States now, would have been even more so in 1962. But of course Baldwin didn’t hold back when he had something to say, and this book has plenty to say. 

Aside from the discussions this book will provoke, I also loved the way it was delivered. Reading his prose is such a treat. The fluidity of this narrative as it zooms around New York, from character to character and scene to scene; the feeling of a jazz record always playing in the background; the witty and sharp dialogue countered by honest and heartfelt confessions. It all adds up to a memorable and impactful reading experience, perhaps my best of the year so far!
