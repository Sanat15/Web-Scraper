Rating: 5/5

The way I see it, the novel tackles multiple genres that were brewing at the time of its publication, which resulted into one of the richest and most beautifully sardonic novels of the 17th century. The Simplicius tells the story of a boy with no name, who grows to become a man of many names and who ultimately renounces the necessity of one once he realizes how disappointing, amoral and cyclically self-destructive humanity really is. Not that he swam in the other direction for most of his life, he benefited from exploiting others himself, but at what cost? And to what purpose? We have love in our hearts and yet we waste it all in mortal matters that do not serve any purpose. From dust we came and to dust shall we return, yet most of us cannot grasp this simple idea. 

The Simplicius is a masterpiece. One of the greatest novels ever written, and not just from the 17th century, and not just in the German language. It takes the Spanish tradition of the picaresque and the spellbinding potential of the surfacing utopian genre and does something amazing with it. 

Place this book next to your copy of Faustus in your library.
