Rating: 4/5

This book was essential for me to read, and I urge anyone else who is the parent of a distance runner to get hold of a copy. 

I am the mother of a very young distance runner - a girl who likes to run in the woods and over the hills when nobody is watching. She also likes to take part in races, in which she excels. She trains twice a week, for an hour and a half at a time. Other girls who train much more than her are better than her at the moment. After reading this book, there is no way on earth I will let her train more.

Although this book deals with the 1970s and 1980s, many of the pressures put on young athletes have no changed, and perhaps have even intensified. My family do not live in the US, so to look at the scholastic athletic system there is eye-opening to me. Popular online running forums discuss the middle school and high school cross country meets and the performances of individual teenagers as if they were public property. Young runners in the US are under scrutiny in a way that just does not happen in the UK. I am extremely happy that the media and the public at large have no interest in teenage XC, and indeed there are strict guidelines at a club and national level to avoid that happening.

The US system seems like a horrible grinding machine. Excess mileage and insufficient food intake may produce teenage distance stars, but if the cost is long-term mental and physical injury, then it is too high a price.
