Rating: 4/5

I didn’t expect this book, The Rise and Fall of Adolf Hitler by William L. Shirer to be so absorbing.

I had just finished a massive biography on John D. Rockefeller, so when I picked up The Rise and Fall of Adolf Hitler, it felt almost like a palate cleanser. Shorter. Faster. More like an introduction. I figured I’d breeze through it.
I was wrong.
The book is disturbing, sobering, and unexpectedly gripping.
What initially pulled me in—oddly enough—was a stand-up comedian’s joke. I think it was Dana Gould, who quipped that Hitler finally got married… and within about 48 hours, he killed himself. The joke lands because marriage is hard—but it also made me wonder: what’s the real story here? That curiosity pushed me to read the actual history.
And the reality is far darker than the joke.

This book made me realize just how little value Hitler placed on any human life—including his own. The sheer scale of death, not only among Jews and Poles but among Germans themselves, is staggering. He murdered rivals, generals, political opponents, and civilians with chilling indifference. At one point, he ordered the execution of roughly a thousand people in a single weekend simply to consolidate power. That was it. No trial. No remorse.
From a psychological standpoint, he fits disturbingly well with the definition of a psychopath:
someone who lacks empathy, does not value human life, and cannot place themselves in another person’s suffering.
Hitler even said as much in his own words—human life, to him, was expendable.
One of the most unsettling ironies is that everything looked “over” for him early on. He wasn’t even German—he was Austrian. He failed as an artist. He went to prison for nearly five years. His political movement was crushed. And yet prison became the turning point. While incarcerated, he wrote Mein Kampf (originally intended to have an absurdly long title before being shortened to My Struggle). The book sold massively, made him wealthy, and—most importantly—made him famous. It set him apart and resurrected his political life.
Another disturbing detail: according to at least one American journalist, the only person Hitler may have truly loved was his half-niece. That relationship alone says volumes about how broken and isolated he was. He had no real friendships, no healthy love, and no accountability.
Strategically, the book makes clear that Hitler didn’t lose because he lacked power—he lost because of greed and haste.
He gained territory without war through concessions. He made a private pact with Russia. He could have consolidated Europe and stopped. Instead, he did too much, too fast:
He attacked Britain unnecessarily
He invaded Russia far too early
He broke his own non-aggression pact
And finally, he declared war on the United States—despite having no need to
Emotion, ego, and frenzy overtook strategy.
In that sense, Hitler reminds me of Napoleon—brilliant in rise, undone by overreach and winter. He also reminds me, strangely, of Star Wars: Darth Vader executing generals who failed him, stormtroopers enforcing an empire built on fear, and a leader who values power over life itself. It’s not subtle—World War II clearly shaped that universe.

The ending is as bleak as the life. Hitler descended into paranoia and madness, refusing to listen to his generals. Men like Rommel—who had served him loyally—were forced to choose between execution and suicide. Hitler himself ultimately shot himself in a bunker as Soviet forces closed in, intent on taking as many lives with him as possible.

That’s the opposite of leadership. And it’s the opposite of faith.
What struck me most, as a Christian, is the contrast:
Hitler demanded others die for him.
Missionaries, saints, and Christians throughout history have died for others—bringing medicine, education, compassion, and hope.

We casually throw around the word “Hitler” today, calling anyone we dislike a Nazi or fascist. This book is a reminder of how absurd that is. Hitler wasn’t just authoritarian—he orchestrated the industrialized murder of millions and wanted the world to burn with him.

I listened to this audiobook while walking at the South Premium Outlets, and it turned out to be one of the most unexpectedly powerful listens I’ve had. It’s not an easy book—but it’s an important one.
Next up: an American president.
