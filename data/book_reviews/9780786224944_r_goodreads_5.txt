Rating: 3/5

I do very much and firmly believe that I can certainly claim and point out with I think more than adequate truth and personal confidence that the absolute best parts of Ellis Peters' utterly delightful Brother Cadfael series of Mediaeval mysteries are generally and usually both Peters' spot-on and historically accurate sense of historical time and geographic place (how she makes Mediaeval 12th century England come both realistically and imaginatively, spectacularly to life) and equally so how Ellis Peters also manages to present historical mysteries which might include mayhem, murder, revenge, nastiness and the like but consistently in a textual manner where albeit the stories are always more than sufficiently descriptive and detailed, Peters' Brother Cadfael stories are (and happily so for me personally) never overtly, exaggeratedly horribly or gratuitously violently depicted, presented and told (not to mention that often if not even generally so in the Brother Cadfael series, the mysteries, the necessary whodunnit aspects thereof in fact and appreciatively so seem to come a trifle secondary to the day to day happenings of Shrewsbury and Brother Cadfael's home abbey of Saint Peter and Paul, and which I for one absolutely and hugely appreciate and majorly enjoy, and furthermore, how the English history parts Ellis Peters provides in and with her Brother Cadfael mysteries, these are superbly authentic, these are realistic and as such solid historical fiction and not annoyingly fantastical). 

But yes, I do have to admit that the twelfth Brother Cadfael mystery, that The Raven in the Foregate (1986) is, even if Ellis Peters' sense of time and place is once again outstanding (see above), not one of my favourite series novels. And my biggest issue with The Raven in the Foregate is quite simply that the priest, that Father Ailnoth, whose death Brother Cadfael and Hugh Beringar are investigating is depicted by Peters as such a cardboard thin and stereotypical villain (and whose death is as such also quite deserved) that both when I read The Raven in the Foregate in 1988 and equally so when I reread The Raven in the Foregate in 2026, I have found much of Ellis Peters' text somewhat tedious and thus also not at all engaging. And furthermore, the romance element of The Raven in the Foregate feels pretty frustratingly repetitive as well and it reminds me far too much of the love story found in One Corpse too Many (but that while in the latter, this was a lovely and delightful story, having basically rather the same set-up in The Raven in the Foregate just feels annoying and also somewhat lazy of Peters). 

But while Ellis Peters' plotting of The Raven in the Foregate is thus somewhat thin and that The Raven in the Foregate is definitely not a Brother Cadfael story that truly shines for me, yes, I still have found The Raven in the Foregate sufficiently readable and pleasurable for a solidly three star rating. For Peters with The Raven in the Foregate also does make a wonderful as well as an essential contrast between the graceless and ruthlessly following Church regulations above basic humanity Ailnoth and the ways Hugh and Cadfael approach his death, seeking the truth to be sure but without ever jumping to conclusions, seeing all those who could be suspects (and in their full humanity). There is therefore a commentary in The Raven in the Foregate about how law should be administered (both in church and in society), and that in Brother Cadfael, we see Ellis Peters show his devotion to God and in Hugh his devotion to King Stephen. But yes, both are also shown in The Raven in the Foregate as pursuing their paths with an eye for both justice and mercy and as such very differently than the hapless and also pathetic Father Ailnoth, whose complete lack of mercy actually causes his death (with him drowning in the Mill Pond after slipping into the water while being nasty and judgmental, with no-one willing to help since in the short time Ailnoth had been the Foregate priest, he had basically at best alienated everyone and had also caused deaths due to his cruelty and lack of any and all humanity and humility).
