Rating: 4/5

4.25 ⭐️ (round ⬇️) — Witches, Giants &amp; Banshees.. Oh my! This was a delightful read that I really enjoyed reading in very small 5-10 page bites, across the weeks since August. This serial reading I think really help make the lose all the more elegant, impactful &amp; helped it stay with me as often I’d read this pages before getting out of the car at work, meaning the magical wonder of fairies &amp; folklore was permeating my consciousness throughout the day, lingering, adapting and often finally clicking in many cases where I’d thought I’d never quite crack the allegorical message or the sentiment being expressed.

As a collection, Irish Fairy and Folk Tales is a masterful ode to the oral traditions of Ireland. This book is not just a series of stories—it’s an echo of a culture steeped in myth, magic, and a kind of wistful melancholy that lingers long after you turn the final page. While uneven in places (some tales feel more like fragments than fully realized narratives), the raw, uninhibited beauty of the prose &amp; visceral sense of Ireland’s folklore make this a treasure worth savouring, with marginalia-a-plenty a must for an intoxicant lien moi!

What struck me most is how these tales influenced literary giants like W.B. Yeats, James Joyce, and even contemporary writers. The storytelling tradition captured here forms the backbone of so much Irish literature, intertwining nature, history, and the supernatural. In “The Brewery of Eggshells,” the line between the ordinary and the magical is paper-thin. The prose crackles with life: “‘You have done what I bid you,’ said the wise woman. ‘And now you have proved that you were right to doubt. Your child is gone, stolen by the fairies.’” That quiet devastation feels timeless, a reminder of how folklore reflects human fears and resilience.

The poetry interspersed throughout also deserves praise. Lines like these from “The Fairy Host” struck me: “But the mystery and the gloom have fled from the woods and the streams; / The music is low in the glens, and faint in the mountain dreams.” It’s this tension between beauty and loss that gives these tales their enduring power.

My one quibble lies in the lack of consistency; some entries feel overly cryptic or underdeveloped. Still, there’s magic here—layered, ancient &amp; endlessly evocative. For anyone who wants to hear the heartbeat of Irish myth, this is a book to cherish. You may find yourself reading it aloud, as it was surely meant to be shared.

Reading this has only strengthened my desire to visit Ireland and Northern Ireland someday. I dream of wandering the lush, green countryside, soaking in the misty fog of Belfast’s historic shipyards, steeped in lore and industry. This book captures the soul of a land I long to see—and you might, too.
