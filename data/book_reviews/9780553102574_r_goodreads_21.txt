Rating: 5/5

Such a good book! 

It is, as advertised, a novel of suspense. It deals with the horrors of slavery. But it's full of hope and humanity.

In a way, the title doesn't make a lot of sense. Benjamin January was never actually sold down the river, into the killing labor of the canefields of Louisiana. But in a way, he thinks, his mother sold him down the river when she gave birth to him on such a plantation. Now a free man, he's returned to solve a mystery, not to that plantation but to another owned by the same brutal master who beat him unconscious when he was a child. He's not doing it to help that slaveholder but rather the enslaved people there, who might all be killed if he doesn't figure out who's wreaking havoc with poisoning and sabotage. 

Things get pretty hairy - at one point January finds himself praying, asking Mary to help him kill an innocent man to get a horse he thinks he needs to save his life. His prayer shows him what he's doing and changes his mind and his plans.

At an explosive point in the story, January's surprisingly learned tobacco-spitting American friend Abishag Shaw, says, "Was it Seneca that said Death is Freedom for a slave?" Sometimes it is. 

I love the way all the people of color look out for each other and help each other, as do a couple of white allies. Still, what he has seen gives January a lot of anger and hatred toward white people for a while afterwards, enough that he seeks help from his confessor. 

This book would have greatly benefited from a list of characters at the beginning. Except that might have interfered with the author's intent to show you who's who gradually and foggily, as January learns about them on the cane plantation. What I should have done, and plan to do when I read future books in this series, is to make my own list. I also resolve to read them MUCH more slowly. I read way too fast, because it's exciting, and I miss too much, and get confused.
