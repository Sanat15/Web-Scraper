Rating: 5/5

Once, during the Partition riots, a Hindu man broke down before Gandhi and confessed: “I killed a Muslim child. I picked him up by the legs and smashed his head against the wall. There’s no hope or redemption for me.” Gandhi replied that there was still a way to atone: he should adopt a Muslim orphan and raise the child as his own. To Gandhi, no crime, however grave, was beyond atonement.

I’m not sure if this story is apocryphal or real (perhaps I saw it in the film Gandhi), but it came to mind as I read this book by Simon Wiesenthal (yes, the famous Nazi hunter).

The book begins with Wiesenthal’s own harrowing encounter: while he was a prisoner in a concentration camp, a dying Nazi officer summoned him. The officer, through a nurse, asked for a Jewish prisoner – any prisoner – to hear his confession. He then admitted to massacring innocent Jewish men, women, and children, and pleaded for forgiveness. Wiesenthal stayed silent. Later, he was left to wrestle with the question: had he done the right thing?

That single story could have been a book in itself. But The Sunflower goes further. The second half gathers the reflections of others – writers, philosophers, professors, religious leaders. The Dalai Lama, Desmond Tutu, Primo Levi, Cynthia Ozick, among many others, weigh in on the morality at stake.

Some, largely from Christian traditions, argue that forgiveness should have been granted. Others insist that Wiesenthal had no right to forgive, just as the Nazi had no right to ask.

The result is a book that sits at the crossroads of history, philosophy, morality, and ethics, and makes for deeply thought-provoking reading.

And, as I closed the last page, I found myself wondering: what would Gandhi have done?
