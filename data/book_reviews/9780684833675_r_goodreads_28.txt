Rating: 5/5

This is the last of the Space Trilogy. It is the weakest of the series, in my opinion, though only due to the banality of the evil organization, N.I.C.E. and the very slow start. Much of the novel is spent in the hum-drum administrative blather of the organization bent on eliminating humanity and disembodying its leaders and thus granting immortality to the chosen few.

The second half of the book is a page-turner, like the previous two volumes, and I don't fully understand why Lewis chose to make the first third of the book so slow. I have no doubt he did it for a reason--perhaps to slowly transform the familiar into the fantastic that we so often look past with our modernist lenses.

The novel is redeemed by the rich mythology that persists and is ennobled from the previous two books. Lewis has created a convincing and gripping mythology that fits so convincingly into our own world--blending seamlessly with British mythology.

It is unlike Narnia or Middle Earth, in that neither of those books really convince us of the reality of their worlds. But Lewis' world is entirely convincing, almost convincing one of the reality of life outside of earth, and earth's history as a mere piece in the history of a solar system teeming with life and the creative power of God.

I highly recommend this book and the whole trilogy.

EDIT 10/09/2021

Having now read through this for a third time, I no longer consider this the weakest of the series. In fact, it is now right behind "Out of the Silent Planet" in my opinion.
