Rating: 4/5

This book was published in 1957 and thus gives a unique perspective one would not get from a more recent book. The parts of this book dealing with geology were dry (“tedious” is the word another reviewer used for this book, and I agree that those sections were tedious), though that is not the fault of the author as geology is HARD. But there were portions that were absolutely fascinating, particularly the history of John Wesley Powell and his first expedition mapping the Colorado River. In the last hundred pages it became very clear that the author is what we would now call an environmentalist - a huge advocate for maintaining wilderness land/ national parks and monuments. Apparently at that time, there were many attempts to develop those areas, and I wish the author was still alive to know that they’ve been maintained as wilderness, despite what I consider laudable advances to make parts of them accessible to those unable to do the kind of hiking I love to do. A couple quotes: “The wilderness and the idea of the wilderness is one of the permanent homes of the human spirit.” And, about the author’s “collection” of plants and flowers, “the more one knows, the more likely one is to observe something he knows he does not know.” I learned a lot reading this book; read it on a 2-week camping trip which started with 3 nights and 2 days in the Grand Canyon, which seemed an appropriate time to do so. I brought it from Maine to Phoenix and will leave it in a Little Free Library here, hopefully for someone else of my ilk to enjoy.
