Rating: 4/5

When "Fugitives' Fire" (1928) begins, we are introduced to Paul Torridon, aka White Thunder, a white teen who was captured by the great Cheyenne warrior Standing Bull, now living as a prisoner among the Cheyenne tribe. Constantly under guard, White Thunder is being kept happy and rich by the superstitious Cheyenne because he has been bringing them good medicine/luck since being captured. The opening sequence is quick and smart as Brand settles us into fun conversations between White Thunder and those around him. He's wryly half-laughing off Cheyenne ways while turning them against his captors, infusing them with a hidden dose of common sense, suggesting things in ways that earn him his reputation as an indispensable asset for the tribe against misfortune.

White Thunder's interactions with the chief's old wife Young Willow are the best, this young smart alec taking grief from the old crone who keeps him in his place while both are impressed by the other's quick wit. Standing Bull's internal conflict is also notable here as he wants White Thunder to accept his place in the tribe though he knows he only wants to escape, and is paranoid that his own place in the tribe is on shaky ground as long as White Thunder's abilities are so respected by the tribe.

The final third is (semi-spoiler alert) when White Thunder and his girl finally find their chance to escape and it is a tense race into the plains, a smart manhunt and struggle for survival. This sequence is, so far in my reading, my favorite Max Brand western narrative.

Verdict: "Fugitives' Fire" is an old but relevant, well-written and fun Indian adventure from Max Brand.
