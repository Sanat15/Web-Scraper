Rating: 2/5

"Smells of the lamp," remarks Harriet at one point. On another occasion she bridles at having her characters make love to each other (making love '30s-style, that is). It's hard to avoid the impression that these are Dorothy L Sayers's covert howls of protest at a story that's boring her to tears. She's brought back Harriet Vane but doesn't know what to do with her. (It's not until she writes the part of Hilary Thorpe in The Nine Tailors that the lightbulb goes on and she sees where to fit Harriet in.)

The trouble is, this is a sudoku of a mystery, and we know Sayers can do much better. There's no real reason for us to care about the murder - if it is a murder. Sayers toys with having Harriet accused of the crime, but then realizes she already did that in Strong Poison and it's a dead end. Lord Peter runs to and fro being frivolous and annoying, now and again bristling in a manly way as Sayers feels she must jettison his silly-assness if he's ever to be a suitable suitor for Harriet.

I learned a bit about codes and forgot the solution to the crime almost as soon as I closed the book. Peter and Harriet allude to the uncertainty that their recondite solution will lead to a successful trial. Sayers must have felt like she was pulling teeth writing this book. Fortunately, she found a fresh enthusiasm for the characters in later novels. Just don't start with this one or you'll never go on to the high point of Gaudy Night.
