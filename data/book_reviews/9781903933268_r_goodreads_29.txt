Rating: 3/5

This is a difficult book to review.

Darwin waited until this book to publish his thoughts on human evolution. (Had he expressed them earlier, Christians would have mocked him.) In this book, he discusses the ways in which animals speciate from common origins, applying this to human beings, and using examples drawn from anthropology. Alas, in the modern world, the book reveals the limits of his understanding.

Darwin died seven decades before DNA was discovered. Thus, his thoughts on inheritance were speculative. Frequently, he compares 'savages' with 'the civilised', citing cultures as marks of status. He discusses everything from breeding rates to aggression, from social graces to immunity. Quickly, it becomes clear that Darwin was a racist and a White Supremacist.

What also becomes clear is his compassion. Unlike the racism of the Far Right, borne of contempt, Darwin's was borne of pity. True, he thought the White Man superior, but believed He should assist 'savages', drawing attention to commonalities, echoing sentiments of Kipling, who wrote of 'the White Man's Burden'. (It's pleasing to read that, despite his racism, Darwin was an abolitionist.)

This compels us to examine a political point. Too often, fascists refer to Darwin, suggesting that he would approve of segregation and/or genocide. For all its errors, 'The Descent of Man' proves them wrong. Darwin considers emotional faculties found in all 'races', resenting those who would make beasts of men. His central thesis, in essence, is that it would be a rejection of evolution itself.

As scientific writing, 'The Descent of Man' is obsolete - but, as history, it's priceless. Reading the work of the greatest biologist of his age should teach us where we were, what we knew, and how far we've come. Some readers might question the efficacy of reading such material, but I would argue that, precisely because of its faults, 'The Descent of Man' puts paid to its prejudices.
