Rating: 5/5

I loved the breadth and depth of this work by a 32-year-old English professor. Survival (1972) helped me learn a lot about Canadian literature with an entertaining but challenging voice that is preternaturally gifted, candid, and generally (to quote Michelle Yeoh) remarkable. Atwood wrote this overview because her classroom needed a guide to Canadian literature and none (at the time) existed. While it still holds up as a teaching tool, Survival has also become a model for how other nations can think of their own literature that continues to shape them. As she writes in the introduction, “Literature is not only a mirror; it is also a map, a geography of the mind.” 

It is interesting to read the original without any 21st century updates. “He” is the default pronoun, and while Atwood admits her less than expert reading of Québecois literature, the absence of Indigenous stories is hard to swallow. She does have a chapter called “First People: Indians and Eskimos as Symbols,” which focuses on how White settlers projected their fantasies on Native people, a dynamic that is really developed in Toni Morrison’s Playing in the Dark. I appreciated Atwood’s insight on the difference between settler fantasies in the States and Canada. 

All in all, there’s a clarity and honest here that makes these essays timeless. I’m looking forward to a second read.
