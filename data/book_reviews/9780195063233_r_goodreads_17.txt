Rating: 3/5

a really tough one to judge. first off cooper is a truly wonderful writer. i thought i'd hate her more flowery tones over such an imperative subject but it actually just paints such an living breathing picture of what shes talking about. really gifted. 
as for the content, i think i may have nerfed myself a little with expecting this book to be a lot more "modern" in outlook than it was given the time of its publication. cooper is a wonderful advocate for girls and their right to education and to high society, for black americans in every way, and is so, so thoughtful and multifaceted on her reasonings with both of those points that some of her other beliefs kind of took me aback. she was very deeply and sincerely religious, and while she wanted women uplifted to be equal to men, she still stood on the fact that they were fundamentally different from each other, and should be treated as so. which was frustrating as she clearly did not think that way over other social constructs such as race. though, that grace was given mostly to her own countrymen, a strange line of xenophobia and racism even to other groups on american soil through her work leads to what can only be described as more cognitive dissonances. also, and this last point is really just in regards to my own lack of research on this time period, but a lot of the essays in this book deal at length with specific figures, cultural phenomena, and minutiae of the era which have left zero remnants to today and may leave you scratching your head a bit.
cooper is a beautiful and persuasive writer. it's just not the #1 smoothest read for a 21st century feminist.
