Rating: 4/5

I used parts of this book for my dissertation three years ago but I have only now gotten around to reading the whole thing. Though its main focus, naturally, is on the Britpop's vanguard artists (primarily Blur, Elastica, Suede and Oasis), it provides equally insightful commentary on the indie music business, the post-Thatcher UK political landscape and the language of contemporary music journalism, and how the likes of the NME, Select, Alan McGee and Tony Blair were as indispensable to Britpop's architecture as Damon Albarn, Brett Anderson, the Gallaghers et al. The prose is wry and critical but also sympathetic, capturing the imperfect humanity of its protagonists as they navigate the oppressive, drug-fuelled web of the '90s music industry. 

The book's two main faults are a curmudgeonly (and dated) Endpiece—written in 2004, it's mainly used as an opportunity for Harris to grumble about Coldplay and Travis and how rock music simply ain't as good as it was back in my day—and the inevitable content bias towards Harris' more generous interviewees*; Elastica's post-breakthrough doldrums are covered more extensively than is really deserved, whereas Suede's equivalent period is virtually ignored. The biggest casualties though are Pulp, whose inclusion amounts to little more than a token gesture. 

Overall, though, this was a highly engaging read that I would recommend to anyone interested in Britpop or '90s UK pop culture in general.

*the acknowledgements section highlights Elastica's Justine Frischmann, Blur's Alex James and Creation label manager Tim Abbott; the latter is responsible for most of the Oasis and Alan McGee material
