Rating: 3/5

"The Girl from Paris" is the third and final volume in the Paget Family Saga, Joan Aiken's loosely connected tale of a Sussex family spanning the late eighteenth to mid-nineteenth century. In this case, the connections are even looser than in "The Weeping Ash," the second volume. Jumping ahead sixty-some years to 1861, we find Ellen Paget, youngest daughter of tyrannical, selfish Luke Paget, making her way in the world as a teacher at the finishing school in Brussels where she's been a pupil (shades of Villette by Charlotte Brontë). Within a short time, Ellen finds herself a governess to an impossible pupil in an aristocratic family in Paris, where she will come into the orbit of the era's literary and artistic stars before abruptly winding up back in Sussex, where she will try to knit together the remnants of her family. Less a romance than a bildungsroman, Ellen is a likable heroine, but the novel is marked by Aiken's characteristic authorial insistence that her readers never forget she is in control and can throw them a curveball if she chooses...and she chooses. As in "The Weeping Ash," the novel meanders along until it seems as if Aiken is tired of writing it; then, with a few vigorous shakes, she turns it back into a romance but is unable to prevent herself from several spiteful disposals of several characters. If she weren't a good writer, it would be an unpleasant ride, but Aiken's prickliness is part of her charm.
