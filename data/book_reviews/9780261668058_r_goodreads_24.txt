Rating: 5/5

William Butler Yeats is undoubtedly one of my favourite Irish writers, prolific for his stories and romantic poetry, so I had to purchase this book upon my trip to the Irish capital at Hodgis and Figgis-Ireland's oldest bookshop.

Collected and compiled by Yeats in 1888, Irish Fairy and Folk Tales is a masterful ode to the oral traditions of Ireland. This book is not just a series of stories-- some allegorical, others entrenched in fantasy—it’s an echo of a culture steeped in myth, magic, and a kind of wistful melancholy that lingers long after you turn the final page. There are stories of nightmarish creatures called pooka that haunt old ruins, banshees with their spine-tingling wails announcing a death, changelings who are children stolen away by faeries and replaced with their own, giants, heroic princes, quite literal headless phantoms and ghosts, demon cats, witches, devils, and all other manner of Irish creatures of myth and fantasy. 

While some stories appear overly cryptic and even at times with phonetic misspellings representing dialogue, this rich collection nonetheless creates a picture of a nostalgic Ireland steeped in supernatural folklore and inhibited beauty--a place so visceral and enchanting, it evokes a sense of fernweh in your heart. The oral tradition of Irish folklore and myth represented here forms much of the backbone of Irish literature, inspiring great writers such as Yeats and James Joyce and other contemporary writers inspired by an old Ireland entrenched in ancient magic. 

4.5 stars.
