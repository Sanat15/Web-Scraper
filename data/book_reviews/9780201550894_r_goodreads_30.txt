Rating: 2/5

Preety poor. If you're reading it in order to reduce your own procrastination, the first half of the book is completely useless to you. If you're reading it to get a better understanding of procrastination, it's basically no good either, as I'll explain now. The first half is skant, based largely on common-sense-isms, is not rigorous, and deals primarily with personal anecdotes rather than an analysis of the research. It uses a lot of phrases like 'tend to' or 'usually', i.e. generalisations rather than something like 'in a metastudy, Dr. X of Y university found that 56% of procrastinators do Z'.

But if you were like me, you were probably reading it for ways to overcome procrastination, not understand it. If there's any place for the latter in a practical guidebook, it should be a small intro, not half the book. Anyway, once you get to the meat of the book, there's only two or three chapters which are useful to the general public, the rest being for those with time-management problems, ADHD, from different cultures or who don't have procrastination themselves but live with someone who does. The few chapters which actually try to help you deal with the problem are more or less just lists of a few things that are easy to find in a blog article after a quick internet search. That could generally be said about the first half too, you could probably find as much information in a couple of internet searches, and it would be more focused on your specific concern, rather than dedicating a chapter each to different causes of procrastination, many of which might not be relevant to you.

The book is also very long-winded, so you don't really get as much as you think you get, which I think bolsters my view that you'd be better off with some internet searches than this book. I suppose that makes sense given that the book was first published before the internet was common, making it a bit of an anachronism nowadays. In short, I do not recommend this book.
