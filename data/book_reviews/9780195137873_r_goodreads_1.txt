Middle-Class Anti-Communism 101…

Preamble:
--I’ve been working on videos (see @TheConspiracyIsCapitalism) where I walk through step-by-step my preferred methodologies (with plenty of diagrams!). The first series:
i) learns lessons from the “Vietnam War”
ii) applies these to today’s war
--The book I relied on the most was Kolko’s 688-page tome Anatomy of a War: Vietnam, the United States, and the Modern Historical Experience (so, I won’t repeat my big picture summary here). Kolko was very impressed by the Vietnamese Communists’ epic struggle. 
--Elliott’s book was perhaps the second most useful, since it gives a conflicting perspective, Vietnamese anti-Communists, which I’ll focus on here. The pathology of middle-class apathy is a crucial topic to dissect (5 stars for what I learned), even though it’s a frustrating and uninspiring perspective (how many stars to remove?). At least this book is well-written given its readable autobiographical storytelling (optimal for building empathy) interspersed with concise historical overviews. 

Highlights: 

1) Traditional Ruling Class: 
--As my video series details, colonialism/imperialism rely on divide-and-rule, exploiting the contradictions of traditional societies (esp. class conflicts) by bribing the traditional ruling class. 
--Note: we should not assume “traditional” means eternal. Prior to the rise of agricultural hierarchies was hunting-gathering egalitarianism: Affluence Without Abundance: The Disappearing World of the Bushmen
--Elliott’s father comes from the traditional Confucian bureaucracy, providing an intimate view of its contradictions: 
i) Confucian class values: 
--In the Confusion value hierarchy, scholars (mandarins; the bureaucracy) was at the top. Elliott lists peasants next, as they were culturally valued (recognized as the productive base), but landlords obviously had more political power (patron-client relationship; perhaps Elliott just grouped landlords with the bureaucracy). Money activities were seen as parasitic/immoral; thus, artisans and especially merchants were at the bottom culturally. 
--Under this hierarchy, Elliott frames merchants as progressive in challenging the status quo, including financing reforms and allowing women’s education. 
ii) Patriarchy and nepotism: 
--Family, especially the father/husband, commanded loyalty. Elliott provides useful insights as a daughter of a mandarin patriarch. 
--When confronted with French colonialism, the Confucian bureaucracy was split between:
a) Engagement: 
--Elliott frames the ambivalence of some elites, eventually agreeing to modernize like Japan to eventually expel the French, esp. after the brutality of French rubber plantations shatter any illusions of civilizing mission. 
--Elliott describes her father as trying to act as a buffer between his local clients vs. French colonialism. 
b) Withdrawal: 		
--Meanwhile, others tried to keep their distance. Clearly, these elites could not support revolutionary action either as that would also threaten their decaying authority. 

2) Collaboration: 
--Beyond bribing elites, there were important positions for lower-class opportunists:
i) Colonial bureaucracy: 
--French colonialism recruited replacements (previously low status, non-scholars) to fill the colonial bureaucracy, those who can learn French and collaborate as interpreters/clerks. These opportunists rose up the ranks (including bribes/using French law to accumulate land) and were scorned by locals as “houseboys” of the French.
--French divide-and-rule policy left multiple competing regional powers. Weaponizing right-wing identity politics, these groups featured religious sects (Buddhist Hoa Hao, synchretistic Cao Dai) and gangsters (Binh Xuyen: prostitution/gambling/opium trade…typical for European colonialism).
ii) Colonial military: 
--Each of the regional powers had their own private army. The French colonial army was also "bulging with native recruits". 
...For a detailed case study of collaboration by joining colonial military/policing, see: Bound by War: How the United States and the Philippines Built America's First Pacific Century
--French colonialism’s new middle-class:
i) Landlords: 
--Elliott mentions how large landlords were rare pre-French colonialism as villages were more communal, so land accumulation would have to be gradual. 
--With French colonialism came infrastructure projects/plantations/land seizure and usury against peasants/opportunists. 
ii) Commerce: 
--Suppliers for colonial army, distributors of French imports (esp. opium/alcohol), etc. 

3) Middle-Class Apathy: 
--The Great Depression/WWII’s Japanese colonization worsened conditions (esp. 1944-45 famine), with Vietnamese Communists leading the organizing of resistance:
a) the initial focus was national liberation, thus requiring a cross-class alliance uniting the peasant base/workers with the middle class (patriotic landlords/intellectuals, etc.). 
b) thus, Communist leaders had to hold back peasant/radical demands for land reform/social revolution against the landlord class.
…As protracted war’s burden on the peasantry mounted (threatening morale), the Communists could not hold back land reform any longer, finally opening it up at the end of 1953. Elliot describes the contradictions; peasant morale was boosted for the pivotal Battle of Dien Bien Phu, but land reform excesses occurred (where low-class background became valued more than patriotism/contributions to the war). 
…Note how in Elliott's account, violence against higher classes counts for so much more than against lower classes (where it’s normalized). Elliott also describes how land reforms were paired with collective use of farming inputs and social goods (income security/housing/healthcare/education) which increased peasant living standards, although diminished by the war’s drain of resources. 
--Eliott’s family was split:
a) Her mandarin father, while respecting the Communists’ patriotism, still saw their potential social revolution as a bigger threat than French colonialism. As the patriarch, this influenced the rest of the family. 
b) Elliott’s highly-moral sister was an outlier, joining the Vietnamese Communists. Her sister’s husband also joins after his perspective was changed by the famine/meeting Communist leaders (realizing they were educated)/reading Engels’ laws of history. 
--After the Vietnamese Communists repelled French colonialism (First Indochina War), the U.S. stepped in (Second Indochina War, the "Vietnam War").
...The father saw the US as allies rather than colonizers. US imports fueled middle-class consumerism (civil servants/military officers/businessmen/landlords), neglecting agricultural industrial economic development (South Vietnam dictator Diem also prevented land reforms which even the US seems to have supported, given successes in US satellites Japan/Taiwan/South Korea: Seeds of Stability: Land Reform and US Foreign Policy). 
--Crucial to Elliott’s longstanding anti-communism was compartmentalization: 
a) Personal experiences: 
--Elliott respected her moral Communist sister, and the Communists’ role in ending colonial rule and their talent/efficiency. 
b) Abstract ideologies/patriarchy: 
--However, Elliott defaulted to her father’s position: fear of communist ideology being anti-family (replaced by the Party) and anti-nation (replaced by international communism; despite the Party leading national liberation). I did not try, however, to analyze why the Viet Minh had succeeded where other movements had failed, and why they were able to attract and keep people like my sister and her husband. I compartmentalized my feelings: admiration for Thang and Hau and the Viet Minh's victory on the one hand, and fear of communism as an ideology on the other, which was the more dominant of the two sentiments.--This compartmentalization also reminds me of “What is Politics?” episode 12 describing how, in the documentary Encounter Point, Palestinian and Israeli participants were able to resolve their differences on a personal level, but could not do so on an abstract ideological level.
