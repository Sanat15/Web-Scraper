Rating: 3/5

Wall writes this book in a relatively dry and monotone way, however does provide deeper insight into some of the surrounding political, social and economic changes of the day that are not present in other gilded age biographies. I only give three stars or greater for books that I have found worth finishing and enjoyed. This book is certainly among them and is my go to book of the man. I would classify Carnegie's story as one of the most motivating success stories in the history of capitalism. 

I found this book especially enlightening, in respect to how Carnegie became the wealthiest "winner" of the 19th century, while his father was part of a cohort of professionals who ended as the greatest "losers" of the 19th century -- the weavers. The irony is intriguing and speaks to what is/was possible in the American system that we've come to understand as the "American dream". Although an important figure in American history, this book helped me establish Carnegie as more of a gilded age "venture capitalist", with less operational hands-on involvement than his contemporaries.

I also enjoyed how the author put emphasis on Carnegie's philosophical transition throughout his life. In his younger years, Carnegie would have categorized himself as the evangelist for Herbert Spencer's theories on social darwinism. In his later years, Carnegie sees the short-comings in the theory -- from a practical perspective. 

Compared to other figures of the same era, Carnegie isn’t someone I particularly admire, respect, draw inspiration from, or relate to. Yet he is, without a doubt, one of the greats of American and industrial history and deserves that recognition.
