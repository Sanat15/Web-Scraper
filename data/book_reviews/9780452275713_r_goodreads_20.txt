Rating: 4/5

Reminiscent of Hemingway in it's unadorned style. But maybe better for those soul-stirring elegies, like this one at the very end:

"Nothing is ever buried, the earth rolls in it's tracks, it never goes anywhere, it never changes, only the hope changes like morning and night, only the expectations rise and set. Why does there have to be promise before destruction?"

Or this one a few pages earlier:

"...the only thing growing was trouble; and it made me shudder to think whatever perfection was, like the perfection I had with Molly, it was maybe past, silently come and gone, a moment long, just the instant in the shadow of one day, and any fool who was still waiting for it, like he dreamed, didn't know what life is."

Side note: I read this to accompany Stephen King's "The Shining." "Welcome to Hard Times" is conspicuously referenced in King's novel (Jack Torrance, by no coincidence, entertains himself with Doctorow's debut novel while sitting in his truck outside The Overlook). The book clearly influenced King during this period. Both stories detail families led by sentimental and overly optimistic father figures whose relentless pursuit of the elusive "American Dream" is symbolized by a hasty trek Westward, and which in both cases results in self-destruction.
