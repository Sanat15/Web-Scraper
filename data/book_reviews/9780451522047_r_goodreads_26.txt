Rating: 4/5

This is one of the great American classics from the 20s by the author of Sister Carrie which many of us probably read in high school. Ironically, the grammar in this book leaves a LOT to be desired, particularly at the end with over half the paragraphs starting with "And then..." and bizarre, primitive phrasing. The descriptions are not particularly ornate or poetic. However, the story itself is compelling and interesting. I never really got to the place of feeling sorry for Clyde, but I could see the moral ambiguities in his story that the author was trying to bring out. A book about a real murder immediately brings to mind comparisons with similar classics and in particular The Executioner's Song and In Cold Blood. Having detested the former, I can say I preferred Dreiser's more hands-off approach to telling the story over Mailer's more heavy-handed non-fiction approach. A better comparison would be with Capote's masterpiece which I preferred, but felt that both Truman and Theodore truly tried to get into the minds of their respective killers with a great degree of success. 

I suppose that the title is a bit ambiguous as well because it makes the reader wonder whether such a story could be told in contemporary France or England? There is a uniqueness to the gritty evangelist pauper to wannabe socialite in Dreiser's Clyde that would be hard to imagine in another society. The descriptions of Kansas City and Lycurgus, NY were very interesting studies of life as a hotel clerk and of the idle rich in America of the 20s.

As for its enduring legacy, one could legitimately wonder how Arrowsmith beat this one out in 1925 for the Pulitzer. That being said, it might be remembered that momentous The Great Gatsby was also published in the same year and, at least for me, remains on an entirely different and higher plane than either of these in terms of vision, breadth and power.
