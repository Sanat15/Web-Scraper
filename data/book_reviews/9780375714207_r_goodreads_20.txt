Rating: 4/5

Mary Renault had a gift for telling history from the perspective of a single distinct character; in this case, the poet/singer Simonides. Born to a landholding family on Keos, he was an ugly baby marked with a dark birthmark on his face, with siblings who were beautiful. Feeling himself an outcast in his own family, he tended his flocks and made songs to ease his soul... until he heard a visiting praise singer, and boldly asked for an apprenticeship.
It was the age of tyrants, before the word became a pejorative or accusation. Many were patrons of the arts, and Simonides and his brethren were able to make good livings crafting their poetry, keeping the old songs and stories alive committed to memory, performing in festivals, for rites for the gods, and for victors' celebrations at the Olympics and other games.
Simonides witnesses the deaths of tyrants in Samos and Athens, and the dual rules of Athenian brothers Hippias and Hipparchos after the death of their respected father Pisistratos. The praise singer saw Greece just before the Persians arrived... a last glowing time prior to a fall.
If I have to have Greek history, this is how it should be served. 
I've read this at least three times. It will remain on my shelf until I am gone, no doubt.
