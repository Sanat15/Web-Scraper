Rating: 5/5

Namako's narrator is a young girl lingering in the bardo zone between adolescence and adulthood. The young Ellen has been developing all her short life the meaning of her bi-cultural lineage—a Japanese-anglo mother and an American father. Ellen is wise and perceptive beyond her years and perfect for telling this story. What is most beautiful beyond her occasional pain and disenchantment is her lyrical expression. The novel often feels like a prose poem with climactic verses and episodes, some unexpected. Eventually, in biology class we meet the defining symbol, the namako, the sea cucumber: "The brown thing in the jar looked like a long pouch fringed at the mouth with whisker-like tentacles." It was an animal Ellen says to her classmate but named for a vegetable. "It must be horrible to be so strange that nobody knows what you are." This could be analogous to her own in-between two cultures. A stern Japanese grandmother is an important matriarch for Ellen, despite communication difficulties. There is comic relief too, as when her father, Gene, starts singing Buffalo Gal, to Ellen's mother to lighten her sadness and the other three siblings join in. This is in the middle of Japan. This is a sweet story with poignant undertones of melancholy and heartfelt sensibility and much more.
