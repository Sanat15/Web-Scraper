Rating: 5/5

I’m allus glad to see a mon like thee
This is one of those delightful miscellanies, like Maugham’s Ten Novels and Their Authors, in which a great author can often be more at ease than in their major works. The chapters are mostly radio talks, newspaper pieces and book reviews. 

Taylor was one of the great C20th historians. He was Left-wing and this could lead him to say some strange things, as that Britain fought WWII ‘largely imperialistically’. But he was no ideologue; he was capable of criticising the Soviet Union (which most Left-wing intellectuals of the time were very reluctant to do), of lamenting the ‘union of hearts’ that was the Commonwealth, and of praising Winston Churchill. In short he had a powerful intellect governed by common sense – a very unusual and precious combination. He had the insight that allows the best historians to see right to the heart of a question. He wrote fluent, good humoured English, and in these pieces can unbend so far as to be quite funny, particularly in his piece on George VI who, he says, saw it as his role to ‘be consulted, discourage, and warn’.

The essay on Manchester of yesteryear, where he began his career, is interesting – and many of his predictions about the city have sadly come true – but you have to wonder how he managed to write it without mentioning the Irish (especially as he showed a consistent interest in Ireland). This Lancashire patriotism is a new side of him to me, and only makes me admire him the more. Yes, it would have been better if he’d titled the book ‘British’ history, and maybe included a bit less about politics. But it’s just the right thing for a bit of intelligent diversion.
