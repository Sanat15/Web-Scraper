Rating: 3/5

An earlier version of the critically acclaimed The City and the Stars, it feels far-fetched recommending reading this book when the other exists and a good question as to why one would even do so. Well, I liked the cover and didn't know about the connection, but ended up enjoying it more than I had thought even while reading it. 

Partly it's the nostalgia for retro-futuristic visions of sleek minimalistic corridors and vastly advanced ephemeral humans floating through them (not literally). When we got to the forests, eh, I could be reading a fantasy book instead. But then the third grabbed me with such strength that it didn't matter that I had read The City and the Stars previously - I was hooked, floating myself through the pages, enjoying the proceedings. 

I am not going to argue that this is not a more raw version of the tale, but while Clarke may fall victim to many habits of his time (for one, five important men to one important woman? At least not a love interest), he is formidable enough as a writer to add dimensions and psychological draw to his characters and fascination to his world. There is no utopia here, it is all built on deceitful myths and comfortable lies, and no civilization is perfect. 

Even so, as apropos of its time, it is still filled with wide-eyed optimism that cannot imagine humanity totally disappearing up its own self-destructive ass and instead always coming back, stronger and wiser, even if it does take billions of years (remember when we used to be so hopeful?)

Taking place after a maddening span of time that didn't feel quite real, the original influenced me to base my Paranoia RPG campaign on it, no wonder then I still found something to enjoy here. My campaign wasn't nearly as good, but we had our fun. And just look at that cover!
