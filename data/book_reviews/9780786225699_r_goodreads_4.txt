Rating: 4/5

I do very much and firmly believe that I can certainly claim and point out with I think more than adequate truth and personal confidence that the absolute best parts of Ellis Peters' utterly delightful Brother Cadfael series of Mediaeval mysteries are generally and usually both Peters' spot-on and historically accurate sense of historical time and geographic place (how she makes Mediaeval 12th century England come both realistically and imaginatively, spectacularly to life) and equally so how Ellis Peters also manages to present historical mysteries which might include mayhem, murder, revenge, nastiness and the like but consistently in a textual manner where albeit the stories are always more than sufficiently descriptive and detailed, Peters' Brother Cadfael stories are (and happily so for me personally) never overtly, exaggeratedly horribly or gratuitously violently depicted, presented and told, not to mention that often if not even generally so in the Brother Cadfael series, the mysteries, the necessary whodunnit aspects thereof in fact and appreciatively so seem to come a trifle secondary to the day to day happenings of Shrewsbury and Brother Cadfael's home abbey of Saint Peter and Paul, and which I for one absolutely and hugely appreciate and majorly enjoy, and furthermore, how the English history parts Ellis Peters provides in and with her Brother Cadfael mysteries, these are superbly authentic, these are realistic and as such solid historical fiction and not annoyingly fantastical.

Now with regard to The Rose Rent (which is the thirteenth Brother Cadfael novel), Peters' main characters and also the plot itself do not (at least for me) primarily concern themselves with the hostilities between King Stephen and the Empress Maud during the civil war from 1138 until 1153 (although this always and necessarily so exists in the background) and equally so not too extensively with Brother Cadfael's Abbey of Saint Peter and Paul in Shrewsbury either. For indeed, Ellis Peters' story for The Rose Rent deals first and foremost with the lives and the culture of the skilled craftspeople of Shrewsbury (and is as such not intensively and extensively about either nobility or about the monks of Shrewsbury). And while the abbey of Saint Peter and Paul is obviously the premise and also the required background for Peters' plot, since there are in The Rose Rent nefarious moves afoot to prevent the rose rent of the book title for a building that wealthy young widow Judith Perle has bequeathed to the abbey to be paid by the abbey of Saint Peter and Paul to Mistress Perle, how the villain (who in my opinion is rather obvious, since I certainly from the onset, from his first appearance in The Rose Rent never even remotely trusted Judith's cousin Miles and found him creepy, smarmy and icky) will obviously resort to even using murder and vandalism to prevent the rose rent being paid, but that The Rose Rent is as such still and nevertheless basically a story fully and wholly about the merchant class. 

And with Peters showing both as background and in the text proper of The Rose Rent how young widow Judith Perle is rather wealthy because she inherited her father's business (but only because there were no sons), with Judith having learned every aspect of the process of making cloth from wool from her father and that ALL of the men who have an eye on her and her successful operation (who want to marry Judith Perle) are in the same stratum of society as well (her foreman, her cousin, a man with large flocks of sheep, a prosperous dyer and fuller, a bronze smith), we as readers of The Rose Rent delightfully and gloriously get to see and to textually encounter a wonderful and also a historically accurate and realistic portrait of Mediaeval England from the perspective of merchants (and that the whodunit aspect of The Rose Rent also comes a bit secondary to this, which I for one appreciate and that I do hugely adore how with the Brother Cadfael series, Ellis Peters shows all aspects of 12th century British and also often Welsh society and that with and in The Rose Rent her textual eye is focussed primarily on what in today's world would probably be considered the middle class and specifically the upper middle class).

Finally, with regard to my personal reading pleasure for The Rose Rent, while Peters' sense of time and place is and as already amply explained above absolutely spectacular and that the focus on Shrewsbury merchants in and throughout The Rose Rent has also been a total textual treat for me, sorry, but there are some minor bones of textual contention, which, although they are entirely personal and are not really something to actually fault Ellis Peters for, do kind of lessen my reading joy and enough so for me to consider The Rose Rent not a five but only a four star reading experience. For one, I definitely think that Cousin Miles is too obvious as a villain (see above) and that this kind of annoys me a trifle or perhaps even more than a trifle regarding The Rose Rent as a mystery. For two, there is in also a bit too much unhappiness for Judith Perle in The Rose Rent (and that this definitely makes me a bit uncomfortable, for it does seem as though Peters is kind of dumping all this pain and more pain onto poor Judith. And for three, while I can to an extent understand why Brother Cadfael only seems to play a rather secondary role in The Rose Rent, I do miss both him and Hugh Beringar have a more active presence and that this has definitely a trifle negatively affected my reading affection for The Rose Rent (although and this being said, The Rose Rent is still excellent and that my four star rating for what Ellis Peters is textually showing and telling is absolutely and totally solid).
