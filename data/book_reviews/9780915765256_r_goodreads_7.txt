Rating: 4/5

There are three seemingly unrelated thoughts that came to my mind while reading Under the Streets of Nice by Ken Follett, and strangely enough, all three contribute to why the book works so well.

First, sitting here in 2026, it is genuinely difficult to imagine an era without mobile phones, surveillance cameras, GPS navigation, digital banking trails, or the endless web of electronic monitoring that now surrounds everyday life. The story revolves around a bank heist planned and executed during such a time, when escape, secrecy, and deception depended far more on nerve, planning, and human ingenuity than on defeating technology. In lesser hands, a plot rooted in that era could easily feel dated or simplistic to modern readers. Yet Follett manages to make it gripping, believable, and remarkably engaging. The tension never feels artificial, and one cannot help admiring the craftsmanship involved in making such a story remain compelling decades later.

Second, the book itself was written in 1990, and since then readers have been flooded with countless stories involving bank robberies, political assassinations, kidnappings, conspiracies, and elaborate criminal operations. Entire genres have repeatedly recycled these themes in both fiction and non-fiction. Against that backdrop, the fact that this book continues to survive, remain relevant, and still be actively read and discussed more than thirty-five years later is an achievement in itself. Most thrillers fade into obscurity once newer and flashier versions arrive, but this one still manages to hold its ground.

The third aspect is perhaps what impressed me most. Usually, novels of this sort rely heavily on the classic “who-dunnit” structure, concealing identities and motivations until the final reveal. Follett takes almost the opposite approach. From very early in the narrative, the reader is made aware of the hunters and the hunted, the planners and the pursuers. Structurally, that could have gone terribly wrong. Once mystery is removed, many thrillers collapse under their own weight. Yet here, the storytelling is so confident and so absorbing that the absence of secrecy hardly matters. The suspense comes not from who will act, but from how events will unfold.

That, in my opinion, is the mark of genuine storytelling ability. The narrative draws you in so naturally that, even after finishing the book, one feels inclined to revisit certain passages and discuss them with others who have read it. Very few thrillers manage to create that sort of lingering engagement.

My only complaint is that the book is too short. One almost wishes Follett had allowed the story and the characters a little more room to breathe, because by the time one is fully immersed in the world he has created, the journey is already over.
