Rating: 5/5

This was exactly what was needed after a succession of good but fairly serious books (which also may have contributed to the 5 star rating). With a caveat that it’s of its time and place (the US South in the fifties) and that the language and attitudes sometimes reflect that in a way that is difficult to read today, this was often laugh out loud funny with one liners that cut to the heart of King’s struggles to fit the southern lady stereotype beloved of her grandma. It’s all done with a lightness of touch that nevertheless succeeds in illustrating the hideous attitudes faced by young women wanting an education and career and the hypocrisy of a world in which everyone is constantly trying to quietly manoeuvre against its strictures (both legal and moral). Be aware also that she takes no prisoners in the language that she uses around sex that sometimes even took me aback reading in 2026! Apparently King later became more obviously conservative (the fact that as a child she reenacts a scene from The Fountainhead was probably a good hint of what was to come even if she counteracts this by wanting to fall in love with a Bulgarian revolutionary à la Turgenev’s On the Eve) but here we see a collection of characters whose views and actions are more complex and nuanced than the good versus bad that it is easier for us to manage. I’m a bit nervous about reading her other books in case her inner Ayn Rand comes more to the fore, but I definitely will seek out more and hope that I am not disappointed!
