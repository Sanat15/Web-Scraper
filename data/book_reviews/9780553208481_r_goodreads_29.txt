Rating: 5/5

At Play in the Fields of the Lord has a slow beginning due to its complex plot elements and themes. I am glad that I stuck with it, as it falls into one of my favorite subgenres of literature: cultural contact and conflict in remote Latin American settings. In this case, a small family of protestant missionaries joins an existing mission in Amazonia in order to bring the fictional Niaruna, who resemble the Yanomamo, to Christ. Other prominent characters include Father Xantes, the rival Catholic priest who serves the same area, Moon and Wolfe, North American mercenaries who have been brought there to bomb the Niaruna village, and the local caudillo, Guzmán.

Known as a naturalist, Matthiessen offers transformative scenes of nature as perceived by the missionaries' child, Billy. They come as a surprise after chapters spent learning the white adult point-of-view, in which the scenery is stinking, rotting, muddy, buggy, unforgiving, and frightening. 

Unlike some other novels in this genre, the main conflict is not political, even though the mendacious Guzmán and his power and influence inform the novel's plot. The cultural/religious conflict between the missionaries, the Catholic Church, and the Niaruna is the main theme: some characters adapt and change, others grow more rigid in their beliefs. Each contributes to the tragedies that result. 

Other works I've read in this subgenre include Graham Greene's The Power and the Glory, Robert Stone's A Flag for Sunrise, Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, and, for background, the ethnography by Napoleon Chagnon, Yanomamo: The Fierce People. If you like any of these, you are likely to appreciate Matthiessen's skillful presentation of At Play in the Fields of the Lord. As I continue with my lifelong recreational reading, I hope to discover novels in this subgenre written in the 21st century. I wonder what the differences may be?
