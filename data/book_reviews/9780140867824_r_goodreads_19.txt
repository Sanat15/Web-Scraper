Rating: 4/5

Ambiguity of war, being great for the ones who go down into history like Henry V (even though there is a strong sense of futility with him dying shortly after the events of the play) versus the experience of common people, seems the main topic of this historical play
When lenity and cruelty play for a kingdom, the gentler gamester is the soonest winner

An ideal preparation for a weekend in Stratford upon Avon and a performance of the play by the Royal Shakespeare Company. The play is remarkably modern in how it shows foreign war being a handy instrument to bolster the reputation of a young g king of England. What Henry manages to pull off is impressive, remaining calm in the face of terrible odds and reciting rousing speeches on how the lack of men in battle is actually fortunate:
We few. We happy few.
We band of brothers, for he today
That sheds his blood with me
Shall be my brother.

He also manages to end up with Catherine, the daughter of the king of France, even though he just killed her brother. Along the way traitors are executed and leniency is offered at times, including allowing the French to burry their dead. At other times Henry does not mind threatening a town with rape, murder of elderly and babies, to get what he wants. Even for a just king, the means at times are grim Shakespeare seems to suggest.

Meanwhile the common people suffer, partly by their desire for wealth and spoils of war, partly just them being the dead that fill the breach. Kids are even part of the war entourage. Two thirds in the world of the king and the people fighting for him are compressed together in a nightly sojourn through his camp. But after the war this closeness is soon brought back to the old social norms and the common man only just manages to gain mercy. For a tale that is about one of the greatest English victories there is a remarkable eye for human suffering and the price of war, which keeps it a very relevant play even today.
