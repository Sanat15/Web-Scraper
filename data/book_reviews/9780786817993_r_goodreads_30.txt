Rating: 3/5

This is a very simple book, but one I would deeply have loved to read as a younger child. It is refreshingly GENTLE for an adventure story. 

Its main heroes are siblings--brave, frustrated, impatient Kestrel and her gentler, more empathetic brother Bowman. Unlike the usual trope, they aren't orphans! They are in fact very close to their parents and their baby sister. While the main action follows Kestrel and Bowman as they try to fix their cursed city by magical means, we do see their parents taking action in their own, less magical way. While their father organizes civil disobedience in prison, their mother, baby in arm, makes herself a prophetess. The efforts of the family to remain together, to empower and encourage each other, are moving.

The writing isn't spectacular, the adventure isn't unforgettable. What I value about this book is, as I said, its gentleness. Quietly, subtly, the book challenges ableist assumptions about those who are "slow" or "awkward" or even just "different." It criticizes the myth of a merit-based society and of standardized testing. One of my favorite moments was when the father character hears his prison comrades discussing the tests that they fail over and over again, because they don't know such-and-such, and how worthless they are in the eyes of their society because of it--and he helps each of them focus instead on the various things that they ARE experts in, the subjects about which they have great passion and knowledge. Even if that's just how to make a really nice cup of tea. 

The book emphasizes appreciating the value of every person you meet--which sometimes means making the effort to LOOK for their value. Over and over again, that is the message of "The Wind Singer." The adventure is nice, the danger is appropriately dangerous, but the book to me says this: be brave, be gentle, be kind.

We could use more books like this.
