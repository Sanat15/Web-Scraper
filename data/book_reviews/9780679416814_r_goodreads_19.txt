Rating: 3/5

John Updike’s efforts in this novel are uneven, perplexing. I usually am not a fan of historical fiction, and this novel attempts to be both history and historical fiction simultaneously. As always his solemn, condensed prose which is poetry in another setting remains. It is just not put to good use. In his novel The Centaur ( 1963 ) he juxtaposes Greek mythology and the present with more purpose.

He does offer some serious and salient observations about history. The possibilities and limitations of historical writing:
P.177 - “History, unlike fiction and physics, never quite jells; it is an armature of other randomly preserved verbal and physical remains upon which historians slap wads of supposition in hopes of the lumpy statue coming to life”
P. 363 - “ There is real time and narrative time, if they were not different it would take as long to tell a man’s life as to live it.”
