During WWII, how did the notion of what it meant to be an American woman help sustain thousands of female prisoners in their long struggle to persevere and survive the Japanese controlled concentration camps in the Philippines? This scholarly survey is drawn from diaries and memoirs of women of different ages, education, and race; reflecting different socioeconomic, marital, and maternal status; who had different skills and temperaments; and imprisoned in various locations scattered throughout the Philippines. How these women saw themselves before the before the Japanese attacked the Philippines influenced their decisions to stay or repatriate (if those fortunate enough to have that option). After the islands were attacked, the women’s self-definitions also influenced decisions whether to avoid interment, and if so, how to evade the enemy as long as possible. After imprisonment, the women’s notions of themselves influenced how they organized their lives and their days, how they went about camp work, the efforts they made to keep their families intact and alive (or for the military nurses, to keep their units intact). It also impacted how they kept their national identity even as they had to bow to the Japanese, and the sacrifices they made to help others and themselves survive Japanese starvation rations and the host of diseases caused by sustained malnutrition.
