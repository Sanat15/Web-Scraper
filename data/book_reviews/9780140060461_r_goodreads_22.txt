Rating: 4/5

At the beginning, Ginzburg criticizes Bakhtin for trying to get at the world of the common people through Rabelais because few common people read Rabelais, but I'm not sure that Ginzburg does much better in trying to get at the common people through the Inquisition's heresy trial of the miller Menocchio. There are few reliable sources about common people of this era that are not seen through the lens of the nobility, so the records of Menocchio's own words at his trial is rare case of the historical record preserving what a commoner thought and said. But come on. The Inquisitors controlled the process and asked all of the questions. From start to finish the trial tracks their agenda. The accused knows that his reputation and freedom and possibly his life are at stake so he has every incentive to answer selectively and lie. And how representative is a miller? The millers were the aristocracy of the common people with more resources, more education and more time to think than common labors and peasants.

But even if Menocchio is not representative of his class, there are some interesting things to be observed in his story. I was surprised at the level of his literacy and his erudition in expressing his theological ideas. His ideas were quite sophisticated - openly questioning the divinity of Jesus, seeing God as a universal presence that pervades the world and advocating religious toleration. The Inquisition was much fairer than I expected. Their main objective was get him to recant sincerely, failing which they wanted to keep him from infecting others with his heretical ideas. And they were meticulous in adhering to their version of due process and record keeping. It all felt much more modern than I expected going in.
