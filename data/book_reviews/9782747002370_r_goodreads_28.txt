Rating: 2/5

The best Goosebumps stories are made from what makes the best stories in general: emotional conflict between characters. The problem with Scarecrow is that the protagonist is confronted with nothing more than vague pseudo-threats, which she always takes seriously. Not until the end do we see any external force deserving of the main character's reaction, but by then three-fourths of the book has been frittered.

I've noticed this structure in a few other Stine books that miss the mark. Attempting to build a foreboding atmosphere, the loom of danger grows ever-larger as the chapters unfold; but the base of the text -- what is ostensibly the plot, though it contains no notable conflict of which to speak -- is devoted to dry day-to-day activities, with occasional mishaps that build to no overarching drama. 

To use this book as an example, were the protagonist to find the character "Sticks" a repulsive insect whom she cannot suffer one moment longer, she might, through internal motivation, be forced to try everything in her power to get him evicted from the farm; this would produce real dramatic conflict, since he would not easily capitulate, and both characters would be compelled to act in succession until a solution is reached.

As it stands, though, the protagonist does little to solve her perceived problem of scarecrow hauntings until the problem grows unignorable -- which is precisely what it should have been in the first place. If a problem can be ignored by the main character, it cannot fuel the story. Action and emotion are both necessary ingredients; one can't have a story without them.

That said, once the story does arrive, it's not bad. A shame it only covers 30 pages.
