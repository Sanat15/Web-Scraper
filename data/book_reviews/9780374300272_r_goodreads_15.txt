Rating: 2/5

An An Acceptable Time is both the fifth novel of Madeleine L’Engle’s Time Quintet and the fourth of her Polly O'Keefe novels and thus functions basically kind of like a conclusion for both series. And in An Acceptable Time, the basic premise is that Polly O’Keefe and Zachary Gray find themselves repeatedly caught in a tesseract (at Polly's maternal grandparents' home in Connecticut, whom she is visiting for an extended period of time and to where Zachary Gary has deliberately followed Polly), with this seemingly at random and automatically appearing tesseract allowing Polly and Zachary to step back in time three thousand years, where they meet up with The People of the Wind and The People Across the Lake, two primitive Native American societies with very much differing belief systems (and yes, I do have to wonder a trifle at the inclusion of ancient Irish druids in An Acceptable Time, since for me, there is certainly something rather off and uncomfortably patronising with Madeleine L'Engle seemingly needing to have the highest healers and priest like figures for those two ancient American tribes be of Caucasian and Western European background). And with An Acceptable Time, while there are multiple, obvious dangers and threats involved with Zachary and Polly moving backwards in time (and particularly so for Polly), Zachary Gray himself does not really care about this at all, as he is dying of heart failure and seeks to be cured by one of the ancient past's healers, so that even Polly trying to explain to Zachary that the price for him being "cured" could very well be human sacrifice, and namely her falls on profoundly deaf and totally self centred ears (maybe not all that surprising because in ALL of the novels where Zachary Gray appears, he is depicted by Madeleine L'Engle as massively arrogant and selfish, but this all becomes totally and utterly, horribly one-sided and reaches a point of almost no return in An Acceptable Time).

Now with regard to Madeleine L'Engle's characterisation for An Acceptable Time and its affects in particular on the cadence and movement of the plot, unfortunately and like with ALL of the Polly O'Keefe novels (except to an extent in A House Like a Lotus) Polly O'Keefe is depicted by Madeleine L'Engle in An Acceptable Time as once again being hugely boring, unremarkable, naive and not at all textually compelling, while Zachary Gray is not only (like usual) arrogant and self-centred, his fear of dying also renders his entire being in An Acceptable Time as completely full of both massive tedium and abject negativity, not to mention that with the two ancient American tribes Zachary and Polly meet three thousand years in the past, their individual members are not very well differentiated from one another, and thus also not people one as readers really manages to care all that much about, and therefore (at least for and to me) leaving An Acceptable Time as neither engaging nor entertaining and at best pretty painfully dragging. 

But aside from me just not really textually liking Madeleine L'Engle's presented storyline for An Acceptable Time with regard to themes, narrative flow and indeed finding most of the presented characters (but especially Polly O'Keefe and Zachary Gray) at best kind of textually ridiculous, there are also certain aspects of An Acceptable Time that just are not logical with regard to L'Engle's featured text. For one, why do the Murry grandparents whom Polly O'Keefe is visiting spend the majority of An Acceptable Time coddling and overprotecting their granddaughter with regard to almost everything (including time travel), but seemingly have no issues with Polly being visited and even actively courted by Zachary Gray? And for two, I definitely would want more text time at the beginning of An Acceptable Time to get myself familiar with the setting, with Polly and her grandparents, and well, having Zachary Gray appear pretty much out of the blue and on page two really is majorly annoying (and that yes, my textual frustrations regarding basically pretty much everything about An Acceptable Time, well, my rating can and will for this reason only be two stars, and I also do think that two stars is pretty generous on my account, for I do indeed find Madeleine L'Engle's writing, her story for An Acceptable Time massively weak and majorly unenjoyable).
