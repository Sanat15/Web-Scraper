Rating: 5/5

Although this is the first book in the series, you don't really have to think of it that way, as it is a direct chronological continuation of Neverness.

Danlo wi Sol Ringess is his father's son in every conceivable way—not only by blood, but in spirit as well. Born and raised among the Devaki, he has rightfully earned the nickname the Wild. The only person in his life who gives him glimpses of a world beyond the knowledge and traditions of the Alaloi tribes is his grandfather, Leopold Soli.

The transformation Danlo undergoes is extraordinary. He is thrust into an entirely new world and confronted with the immense accumulation of human knowledge, but also with humanity's cruelty, arrogance, and, why not say it, its foolishness. It is an unstoppable torrent that sweeps away all but the strongest. Through the strength of his mind, his uncorrupted nature, and a measure of good fortune, Danlo shapes himself according to his own principles—principles that feel far more universally just than much of what civilization has to offer.

Philosophy, love, coming of age, personal growth, gods, the past and the future... the universe... mercy, compassion... Oh, Ahira, it is difficult to find one's way through the labyrinth of life—or perhaps even more difficult to remain true to that path once it has been found.

On the other side, we have the Old Father—the alien philosopher—along with Bardo, Tamara, and Hanuman.

Bardo is a character who hasn't changed all that much. He is still the brave, kind-hearted, yet egotistical and self-important man who strives to make both his name and his fortune into something worthy of admiration. Yet, in the end, he always does what is right, regardless of the cost.

The Old Father is an unimaginably important character. Without him and the journey he undertakes, one has to wonder how much hope there would have been for Danlo. I grew to deeply appreciate every chapter that focused on him.

What can be said about Tamara except how tragic it is that life can be so far beyond our control? One battle won and a lifetime of fears overcome can become meaningless in the face of the ruthless actions of those who believe they have the right not only to decide for themselves, but for everyone else as well.

And finally, what can be said about Hanuman? I'll paraphrase: "You saved his life. Now you will be enemies forever." I thought I fully understood that sentence—until I finished the novel.

In the end, I couldn't help but ask myself whether all of our inner struggles are ultimately in vain. Are we, after all, simply what we are—unchangeable at our core—no matter how many castles of sand we build and call life?

David Zindell once again delivers a deeply philosophical novel disguised as epic science fiction. The Broken God is not merely a story about the future of humanity or the vastness of the cosmos; it is a meditation on identity, morality, compassion, and the possibility—or impossibility—of true change. Few authors combine profound philosophical questions with such an emotionally resonant narrative as Zindell does here.
