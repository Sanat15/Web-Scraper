Rating: 2/5

Pitch meeting:
A: Let's make a Woodstock Oral History!
B: Great! I love that period of music!
A: No, it will have less than 20 pages devoted to the music.
B: Wasn't the Woodstock Festival about music?
A: Not according to the people I interviewed.
B: The musicians didn't talk about music?
A: Musicians? Why would I interview musicians?
B: Oh, I see! You interviewed audience members! It's about the festival experience!
A: No, of course not. I interviewed important people like the director of operations and the purchasing director.
B: What did they do?
A: All the behind-the-scenes stuff of producing the festival.
B: Oh! Behind-the-scenes! That will make a great subtitle.
A: But then significantly less people will care about the book.
B: Well, why not interview the people most readers are interested in hearing from about subjects most readers would care about?
A: I don't think you understand. It's Woodstock: An Oral History! What could be bad about that?
B: You mean with next-to-no discussion of the music and very little on the festival experience?
A: WE INTERVIEWED THE PRESIDENT OF THE PORT-A-POTTY COMPANY! WHAT MORE DO YOU WANT?

One-and-a-half stars. It takes half of the book to get to the actual festival and, as ranted above, barely discusses the music. There's probably just as much of Abbie Hoffman--who believed so hard that the music festival should have been political that he incorrectly believes it somehow was political--as there is about music.

Truth be told, even if a book about Woodstock with no music is like a Back to the Future sequel with no time travel, a behind-the-scenes look at Woodstock could have been interesting. This wasn't. I fell asleep multiple times trying to read this book. I actually hold less respect toward Woodstock after reading this book.
