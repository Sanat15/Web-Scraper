Rating: 3/5

Due to lack of time, this review must be concise and to the point, pared-down to the most essential.

The first three books of the total twelve, compose the “first movement” indicated in the title. I recommend that you read three books of the twelve at one time, rather than each of the twelve one by one. Reading only one of the twelve will not give the reader an adequate feel for the writing and the style.

Nick Jenkins writes of his fellow classmates, many boarders at the same house at Oxford University. They share also earlier schooldays at Eton. At the start the year is 1921. The three books of the first movement cover a bit more than a decade. 

Jenkins is our narrator. He is not introspective, but he is keenly observant of people and happenings. He writes with irony and humor. His views are intelligent and keep the reader thinking. He and his fellow companions are knowledgeable in the arts, literature and politics. This makes their views intellectually interesting. They are of the wealthy and well educated circle. Sure, one could call them posh, but we perceive them as human, just like you and me. They party incessantly. This put a distance between them and me. 

Although readers flit back and forth between a large cast of characters, a handful begin to stand out as the volume comes to an end. I leave the book not attached to any of them, but the thoughts expressed and the writing, the passage through the book, kept my mind busy. Interesting ideas are presented in well written prose.
 
Simon Vance narrates the audiobook. His intonations fit the characters. They are privileged and predominantly those of the upper class. This did get on my nerves off and on. Vance speaks clearly and slowly. Three stars for the narration.

I will continue the series, but not right away. I need a pause. The telling is thought oriented, not plot oriented and not fast moving. There is humor, but to grasp it, attention must be paid.
