Rating: 4/5

I finally decided to read this because it was featured in an episode of the first season of Fringe (which my wife, our oldest kid &amp; I are rewatching). I had a brief moment of disorientation in the first chapter as the scene in which this book was referenced was remarkably similar to the opening scene of the book -- and so was the father/son dynamic that formed the crux of their show. 

But the more I read, the more I realized that the Fringe writers aren't the only people who owe a lot to Jonathan Carroll's literary debut. Consider, for example:

--John Carpenter's 1995 Sam Neill vehicle In the Mouth of Madness
--Bill Willingham's uneven 2000s comic book series Fables
--Once Upon a Time, Disney's charming (and still uneven) rip-off of Willingham's comics
--Mike Carey's cynical 2010s comic book series The Unwritten
--William Spencer Browning's bewildering novel Zod Wallop
--the 2006 Emma Thompson vehicle Stranger Than Fiction
--Lev Grossman's smug and endlessly-derivative novel The Magicians

All of these were pretty clearly influenced by Carroll's work. And I can plainly see where the creator of each was inspired by this book -- and why they chose their variations and divergences.

Is it worth reading on its own? Absolutely. But it's impossible not to recognize the inheritance much of modern metafiction now owes to it.
