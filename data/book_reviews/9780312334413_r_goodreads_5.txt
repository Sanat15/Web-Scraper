Rating: 4/5

Book #6 for 2011.

This historical mystery provided some fascinating background on this nation's early efforts at scientific research and exploration as well as seagoing life during that era. It was clear from her straightforward prose style that Druett was an experienced nonfiction writer, but I was relieved that she did not succumb to the tendency to provide occasional info dumps for the reader's edification. Instead she worked the exposition into the narrative fairly smoothly. I was also relieved that the nautical aspects of the story were considerably more accessible to a landlubber like me than O'Brian's Master &amp; Commander was. (Still need to go back and finish that someday. Perhaps after I read a nautical encyclopaedia.)

The puzzle, generally speaking, was not that challenging. However, there were a few points of its solution that I was slow to pick up on. I also have to remember that we modern readers have been spoiled by the information age and a little jaded by movie magic; once upon a time, certain deceptions were a lot easier to pull off. I also thought the shipboard context made for some interesting constraints to investigative procedure.

Druett also managed to create engaging characters, both historical and fictional. I particularly liked the subtle way in which the reader's perception of each character changed slightly as Wiki gained understanding, even though the book was not a first-person narrative from Wiki's perspective.

I would recommend this book to anybody who enjoys historical mysteries or seagoing adventure tales.
