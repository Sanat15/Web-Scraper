Rating: 3/5

It has been awhile (more than a decade) since I read this book. I picked it up after completing undergraduate studies in English literature, which I completed alongside mostly scientific coursework. In some ways, it is sad how few classes one has to take to complete a major, just 9 in my case for a B.A. in English literature at a well-ranked liberal arts school. This represents just under 30% of the minimum course load one typically has to complete to graduate college. Because my English literature studies felt insubstantial relative to my scientific coursework, I picked up this short text to ensure I had some stronger ground in literary criticism. 

Literary Criticism: An Introduction to Theory and Practice is billed as a structured initiation into the modern critical imagination. Bressler aims to inculcate a methodological self-consciousness during reading, meaning that Bressler hopes to make prospective readers aware of how their assumptions about meaning, language, subjectivity, and power shape their response to a text. Despite the neutral framing of this text, the central theme of Bressler's text is ends up implicitly Foucauldian, which, in some ways, is a good representation of how many literature professor instruct their student in modern classrooms (at least circa the 2010s) despite some of the issues it creates for the study and appreciation of literature.

The book opens deliberately with first principles. Bressler distinguishes between "literary criticism," the practical act of interpreting texts, and "literary theory," the abstract framework that makes such interpretation possible. He insists that no reading is theory-free; even the naïve reader operates with implicit assumptions about what literature is and how it signifies meaning. The early chapters therefore function as an epistemological clearing ground: What counts as literature? Can texts possess multiple meanings? What is the role of the reader? These questions are treated as enduring problems that reverberate throughout the subsequent survey of critical schools.

From this conceptual foundation, Bressler turns to a historical overview, tracing the genealogy of literary thought from Plato and Aristotle through Renaissance and Enlightenment figures to the threshold of modern criticism. This survey is not antiquarian. Rather, it establishes that contemporary theory is an accretion of longstanding debates: mimesis versus expression, moral function versus aesthetic autonomy, authorial intention versus textual independence. By situating modern theory within this lineage, Bressler implicitly argues that what appears as twentieth-century novelty is often a reformulation of classical tensions.

The core of the book consists of systematic expositions of major schools of twentieth-century criticism. Each is presented in a consistent triadic structure: historical development, governing assumptions, and interpretive methodology. This formal regularity is pedagogically significant because it invites comparison and foregrounds the fact that each school constitutes a distinct "way of seeing" rather than a mere set of conclusions.

He begins with Russian Formalism and its Anglo-American counterpart, New Criticism, both of which seek to establish literary study as a quasi-scientific discipline grounded in the formal properties of the text. Here literature is treated as a self-contained object where meaning arises from internal structures, such as imagery, paradox, ambiguity, rather than from authorial intention or historical context. Yet Bressler is careful to show the limits of this approach, including its bracketing of history and subjectivity invites reaction.

That reaction emerged in reader-oriented and structuralist paradigms. Reader-response criticism relocates meaning in the encounter between text and reader, emphasizing subjectivity and interpretive plurality. Stereotypically, reader-response ideas are most often parodied by those skeptical of the rigor of most literary studies in today's academe. Structuralism, influenced by linguistics, posits that meaning is generated by underlying systems, such as codes, conventions, binary oppositions, that structure both language and narrative. In both cases, the stability of the text as an autonomous object begins to dissolve.

The subsequent movement into post-structuralism, most notably deconstruction, marks a decisive and lasting rupture. Here, Bressler introduces the unsettling, somewhat Wittgensteinian claim that language itself is inherently unstable, that meaning is often deferred, indeterminate, or internally contradictory. The text becomes not a vessel of meaning but a site of endless play. Although the post-structuralist position takes structuralist claims to their logical ends, its extremity and deliberate provocativeness sum to a juvenile twaddle that, unfortunately, has dominated so much of the humanities. The epistemic flexibility that poststructuralism authors naturally invites activism into the ivory tower, where they can appropriate clerical legitimacy in order to lay claim to the social levers of power. Bressler fails to address this obvious trend, but it is beyond the scope of the work.

Parallel to these linguistic and philosophical developments, Bressler surveys a series of ideologically inflected approaches: psychoanalytic criticism, Marxism, feminism, postcolonialism, African American criticism, queer theory, and ecocriticism. He fails to connect these to poststructuralism, but at least the lumping of these approaches helps implicitly underscore their triviality. Each of these frameworks expands the field of literary study well beyond the text to encompass broader structures of power, identity, and culture. Literature is no longer an isolated artifact but a node within networks of ideology, such as gender hierarchies, economic relations, colonial histories, environmental consciousness.

What unifies these diverse approaches, in Bressler’s presentation, is not agreement but contestation. Each school defines itself in part by rejecting the assumptions of its predecessors (a reactionary cycle): New Criticism rejects biographical criticism; reader-response rejects textual autonomy; deconstruction rejects structural stability; cultural criticism rejects formal isolation. The history of literary theory thus appears as a dialectical sequence, a series of critiques and counter-critiques through which the object “literature” is continually redefined. This framing is loosely true in a pop intellectual history sense, though the progression errantly implies a sort of progression. However, Bressler's timeline fails to examine why these supposed cycles appear to abruptly halt in the 70s or 80s with now real competitor to the myriad forms of poststructuralism except from conservative thinkers outside the academy who rail against the errors of poststructuralism. 

A distinctive feature of Bressler’s text is its emphasis on method. He operationalizes all theories presented. Each chapter culminates in a set of interpretive questions and often a sample analysis, demonstrating how a given theoretical lens can be applied to a concrete literary work. This practical orientation underscores his central pedagogical claim that theory is not an abstraction imposed upon texts but a toolkit that shapes the acts of reading and writing about literature. However, this nuance walks along the edge of logical incoherence. 

Bressler resists the temptation to adjudicate among competing schools, which in some ways depletes the text. The Humanities has suffered because it refuses to defend its primary intellectual tradition, which is not value-free. It has sought to create an ever-expanding circle and become a flaccid and flabby mess without meaning. Bressler pays too much homage to this literary pluralism. The redeeming value is that he at least foregrounds that reading should be methodologically rigorous.
