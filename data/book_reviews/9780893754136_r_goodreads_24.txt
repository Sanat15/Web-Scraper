Rating: 3/5

Writing in the late 1800s made this novel possible. As magazine serialization grew in popularity, the novel adapted to this new source of income for writers. Long descriptive passages of feelings and setting were sacrificed, and the story was moved forward through succinct events that were sequentially published in the literary magazines of the day. This is how Kidnapped was created, and indeed, the novel was serialized in Young Folks magazine from May to July, 1886.

The creation of Kidnapped for serial publication makes this novel entertaining. At its core, this is a story of adventure. The adventurer is David Balfour who finds himself parentless at the age of 17. Given that he has no prospects in his own small, lowland Scottish town, he sets out to find his uncle in a town located near Edinborough. Upon finding his uncle, he discovers a secrete that his uncle must keep buried. Hence, the uncle arranges for David’s kidnapping and from there the adventure ensues, chapter by chapter..

The words that come to mind after reading Kidnapped are swashbuckling and galavanting. It’s a novel that kept me interested because the situation was constantly changing. However, it’s also simplistic. Beyond the adventure there is not much of anything else. The character are all true to who they are from beginning to end. Another issues was that the novel’s action peaks early on and then it justs sort of coasts to its not so exciting ending.

Lastly, for the Jack London fans among us, the parallels with David Balfour’s kidnapping and the kidnapping of Humphry Van Weyden in London’s The Sea Wolf are uncanny. From “nursing a bruised limb in silent agony,” to being put to work as a cabin boy, to having money stolen from the victim by shipmates; it would be interesting to find out if London was inspired by RLS in his writing of The Sea Wolf some 18 years after the publication of Kidnapped.
