Rating: 3/5

Not an easy book to read, thus not an easy book to review.

David Beresford was a reporter for The Guardian and spent 3 1/2 years in Northern Ireland, much of it during the 1981 Hunger strike. The book consists of diaries, letters, and other communications smuggled in and out of the prison. It was hard, mentally, to read because a) the subject and b) the language of Ireland, which I found hard to comprehend and follow.

Beresford concentrates mostly on the prisoners, all of Republican mind. Try as he might to tell both sides of the conflict, it was difficult when those from the Protestant side would not allow him access to their thought and convictions. 

It is hard not to have sympathy for the prisoners as they protested, and most of the time, Thatcher's oppressive rule and absolute conviction not to negotiate made my blood boil. On the other side, I felt the leadership of the Republican groups could have backed down, and felt it somewhat concerning that 10 men had to die before the strike was called off. 

Like every other book I have read on "the troubles", I never took any side and tried throughout to remain open-minded. This is more of a book meant for scholars of the subject. If you are searching for some background and if you are intent on reading more about the conflicts in divided Ireland, I would recommend Say Nothing: A True Story of Murder and Memory in Northern Ireland before recommending this book.
