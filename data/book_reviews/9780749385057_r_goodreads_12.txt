Rating: 2/5

I approached this novel as I would a tray of baklava. My anticipation verging on lust. As a mouth can water, my mind did, thirsting for beauty and romance. I was cheated.

The Greeks came off as stock characters. The Italians, too. The history is researched. Yes, the author did that. But events never come fully alive. The fault is the writing. Here, for example, is a battle scene. Just try reading this aloud.

"The men of the batteries, demented and disorientated by the mechanical scream of the Stukas, the hail of machinegun fire, and the sticks of bombs that fell amongst their guns and showered them with earth and exiguous shreds of the flesh of their comrades, struggled to remove their limbers and prevent the detonation of their ammunition."

Where do I begin? For stereotypes, we have "demented" soldiers. Cliches include "hail" of gunfire and bombs that "shower" down. These bloat the passage. Remember. It's just one sentence. One of many in this book. Then, the author has to flex his vocabulary. Ah, yes. We are supposed to be impressed. Humbled even. Not this reader. "Exiguous shreds of the flesh of their comrades"? Call guts, what they are. 

What a disappointment this was. And NOT so very original. An Italian director by the name of Gabriele Salvatores made a movie on the same theme. It was called "Mediterraneo" and won best foreign picture in 1992. It is superb. 

Also, in the early 1950s the Italians wrote about the need for this story. Censorship stopped them getting it out. It was well known in Italy. 

This is more than coincidence. Louis de Bernieres researched his material. It shows. It also lacks the beauty of originality.
