Most readers of this anthropological study are likely to be drawn to the tabloid anecdotes—how the Ache abandon their elderly to be picked off by jaguars, or how their women take multiple lovers prior to menarche, etc. But this is not Mondo Cane; the author performs enough post-colonial finger-wagging for us to enjoy these alteric sensationalisms without much guilt, even if some may be warranted. (Clastres is doing the Ache a great service by witnessing and recording the decline of their primitive values in the midst of that great cancer, settler culture. Isn’t he?) Clifford Geertz complained that Clastres’ book, if taken as a study, is weak: it lacks a coherent approach, makes too many assumptions, and prefers to decode signs when perhaps it should be leaving Western ideas of “meaning” behind. These are plausible criticisms.

But I prefer to read this as an odd novel, perhaps a roman à clef. Amidst all the ceremonial mutilations and the whippings with dried tapir penis, Clastres reveals himself as something more than a mere structuralist sniffing out patterns of internal logic and bemoaning cultural exploitation: he is a prose stylist. His text is lean, muscular, and appears to have been built only for the (field)work it is supposed to do, but don’t mistake this for a lack of expressiveness. Clastres can retell a myth in simple but compelling terms that remind me of Willa Cather’s Death Comes for the Archbishop. (I assume that the matter-of-fact exactitude of the diction is the author’s and not that of the translator, Paul Auster.) His book doesn’t offer a coherent narrative, but even the lapses into hermeneutics have the sturdy, image-driven lucidity of great storytelling. The words gleam like cool gems. The chapter divisions—one is called “The Life and Death of a Homosexual”—are clear enticements. And the Ache are patiently sketched, finely rendered characters. I never tired of their coati hunts and child sacrifices.
