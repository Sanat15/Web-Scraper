Rating: 4/5

Of all the things of which I am not fond there are two that apply here: epistolary novels and mushrooms. To me epistolary novels are books which have too many tiny chapters making the plot difficult to follow and, as for mushrooms, I simply do not like them and I never have. You can compare me to George Bush and broccoli - but don't push me on it. They're not animal, they're not vegetable and they seem to thrive on anything in the living world that is decaying or dying - and you want me to eat the fucking stuff!

Never mind - I've calmed myself. This is the story of George Harrison, a man with an amateur devotion for his own uninspiring art and a passion for fungi and he has a wife with a passion for anyone other than George Harrison. In comes John Munting, a literary figure, and his friend, the young talented artist, Harwood Lathom, to the maisonette next door. Things happen. Portraits are painted, friendships made. Then Mr. Harrison goes on holiday to a lonely cottage in Devon without his wife - something he often does - but this time he invites Mr. Lathom to accompany him. Tragedy ensues. One night, while alone in the cottage, Mr. Harrison dies a lonely and distressful death after consuming a dish of his own devising made from edible mushrooms. Was it an accident?

That is the crux of the story and where the authors allow the plot to turn into a detective murder mystery. Is there enough evidence to show foul play or did Mr. Harrison's belief in his own expertise lead him to mistake Amanita rubescans (Warty Caps) with Amanita muscaria (Fly Agaric) or the poisonous muscarine? Then again, did someone else change the fungi before Mr. Harrison cooked them?

Despite the tiresome epistolary format it becomes a fascinating crime novel and well worth reading.
