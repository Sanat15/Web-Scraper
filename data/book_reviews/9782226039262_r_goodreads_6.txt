Rating: 4/5

“Late last night and the night before, tommyknockers, tommyknockers knocking on my door. I wanna go out, don’t know if I can, ’cause I’m so afraid of the Tommyknocker man.”

Well said, Mr. King! This quote still freaks me out. When I was little, I used to mumble it constantly until my mother would throw her slippers at me and scream for me to shut the hell up!

For this Flashback Saturday reading, I chose one of my favorite — and arguably most underrated and misunderstood — works by the king of authors.

The first time I read it, I was eleven. Interestingly, what captivated me most were the broken writer characters with their complicated love-hate dynamic, Bobbie Anderson’s obsession with the mysterious metal object she discovers, and the deterioration of her mental health as her longtime lover/friend/enemy Jim struggles to save her while battling his own demons. My child mind interpreted it as a tragic love story mixed with Close Encounters of the Third Kind.

As usual, there are nearly a hundred supporting characters, each with perfectly crafted and detailed backstories, just like in any King novel. That’s why the book felt thicker than a Dostoevsky novel. Yet somehow my eleven-year-old brain didn’t mind at all. Back then my mind felt clearer, and I could easily catalog every character and storyline without flipping back to remember who was who.

When I reread it years later, I caught references to King’s earlier works, including The Dead Zone, The Shining (even referencing Jack Nicholson’s iconic “Here’s Johnny!” line), and It through mentions of Derry. I also realized how much of Mr. King’s own fragile mental state and personal struggles were reflected in Jim Gardner. At the time he wrote the novel, King himself was battling addiction, and he found the most painfully realistic way to portray Jim’s internal war through his own experiences.

Many readers may dislike the book because of its dramatic ending. Maybe after investing in a novel as massive as an encyclopedia, they expected something lighter and more hopeful. But I think the eerie, foreboding atmosphere King establishes from the very beginning already tells us exactly where the story is headed. Honestly, I believe it was the perfect ending choice.

I still insist this novel is one of his greatest works. It deserves to be reread, reevaluated, and given a far more objective second chance by readers.
