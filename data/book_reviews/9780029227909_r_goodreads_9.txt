Delightfully arrogant, befitting high culture historians. They assume historical thinking is unavoidable and give useful advice for clearing the thicket. Also whet the appetite for history with animated examples.
Summary of practical advice for thinking in time, in short form carried over from appendix a:
1.	Define the decision maker’s situation by listing relevant details and separating known, unclear, and presumed.
2.	Consider analogies, review the likenesses and differences.
3.	Don’t ask ‘what’s the problem,’ ask ‘what’s the story,’
4.	Create a time line for the issue from now to the story’s start. Plot trends and significant changes.
5.	Ask the journalist's question to place details on the time line: when, what, where, who, how, and why.
6.	Ask what odds you give that the presumption proves correct or how much of your own money would you wager on it.
7.	Ask ‘what fresh facts, if at hand, by when, would cause you to changer your presumptions.’
8.	Practice placement for both individuals and organizations. Consider both the private history (a person’s life details, an organization's major events) as well as public history (national and global affairs during a person’s life, relevant public affairs during an organization’s life). This gives you a better operating assumption about the person or organization, but be ready to update that assumption as time passes.
The most important and helpful advice is their chief metaphor, think of time in streams. I editorialize: do not look at decisions as a point of crisis that demands attention and resolution. Instead pay attention to where the issue is coming from, where it is now going, and where/how you can redirect it. A critical insight is that time flows forward inevitably, and no soul can remake it, but a creative person can set it along in a new and previously unimagined path.
